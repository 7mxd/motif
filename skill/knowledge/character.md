# Character

A screen can follow every rule in the design guide and still look as if nobody cared about it: tidy, correct and forgettable. People decide in the first few seconds whether an app feels like what it is, so the brief's personality has to show at a glance. This file says where the brand gets to be loud, how loud, and how to build those moments from the guide's roles. The other knowledge files say what to avoid; this one says what to add.

## The five-second test

Look at a screenshot of the welcome screen for five seconds, then write down three words for how it feels. Compare them with the personality words in `DESIGN.md` section 1.

- If your words match ("energetic, bold, friendly" for a running club that asked for energetic and friendly), the screen has character.
- If your words are "clean, tidy, plain" or "calm, muted" and the brief asked for something else, the screen isn't finished, however correct it is.
- Every one of the brief's words counts, not just the loudest. "Playful, fresh, friendly" for a brief of "playful, fresh, gentle, reassuring" misses two of them. When you compare directions, the one that misses the fewest wins; a full saturated band reads as bold or energetic, never as gentle or calm.

Run it on the welcome or landing screen, sign-in and home, on a phone and (for web apps) a desktop.

## Turn the dial to the brief

How loud the brand gets follows the personality words, not a house style:

| The brief says | Brand moments |
|---|---|
| calm, precise, quiet, professional, premium | A light page with one refined moment per first-impression screen: a well-set `display` headline (in the serif, when the pairing has one, with one word accented), a realistic preview of the product on a raised card, generous space, a short label above the headline. A deep `brand` area at most once: the sign-in panel or the closing band of a landing page, never a heavy block at the top. Quiet everywhere else. |
| friendly, gentle, warm, reassuring | Soft `brand-soft` tints, a friendly illustration on the welcome screen and empty states, rounded icon tiles, warm copy. |
| playful and gentle together (fun words with soft ones: gentle, fresh, reassuring, cosy) | A light page with a soft `brand-soft` wash, a carefully drawn illustrated scene (a mascot is welcome) beside or above the headline, one headline word in `brand`, what the app does as three short rows with icon tiles, rounded generous buttons. No saturated full-width band: the colour lives in the drawing and the accents. |
| energetic, bold, playful, fun, adventurous | A full `brand` band on the welcome screen, a big `display` headline in the heaviest weight the font has, an illustration or mascot, colour-coded content, proof in numbers and faces, lively motion. |

A quiet brief still gets its one moment, and a loud brief still keeps its working screens easy to read. When the words mix rows, the gentler word sets the ground and the louder one shows in the details.

## Where the brand is loud

- **First impressions:** the welcome or landing page, onboarding, sign-in, empty states and success moments. The brand lives here.
- **Working screens:** lists, forms, settings and dashboards. Here the [visual craft](visual-craft.md) rules hold: about a tenth accent, one primary action, `action-primary` only for acting. Character shows in the details: the screen's key number in `display`, today's focus card (the next run, the amount owed) in `brand` with `on-brand` text when the brief is energetic, bold, playful or friendly (a pale card there reads as flat), initials avatars of the people involved, tinted category tiles, an illustrated empty state. For playful, friendly or energetic briefs the dial reaches these screens too, or they read as "clean but plain" beside the welcome screen: each item gets a small drawn thumbnail from the same family as the welcome drawing (the plant's pot with its face, a run's route line), kinds get colour-coded tags, a to-do gets a round check-off circle, and buttons feel tactile (below). This holds at every width: a desktop home keeps the thumbnails and tags rather than switching to a plain table. Items that are places or things with a look of their own (a trail, a park, a plant, a trip) get the drawn thumbnail in any brief but a business tool's, calm ones included: a trail list without its route pictures reads as a plain text list. Each thumbnail is that item's own small scene (dunes for a desert trip, a harbour for a coast, a ridge for a mountain trail), never one placeholder shape repeated down the list. The picture never squeezes the text: on a card it is a short banner across the card's top, or a small square (about 64 px) beside the title; on a phone's list, each card keeps its drawn banner across the top with the status badge ("Closed", "Partly closed") on the banner's corner, then the name on one line, then the figures each led by a small icon; a detail that doesn't fit (a surface type) waits for the item's own page rather than being cut off with an ellipsis; a large picture beside a narrow text column makes titles break on lone words and chips spill onto extra lines. Quiet business tools (invoicing, money, admin) keep their working screens plain.

## The brand roles

Every starting style has three optional colour roles for this; a theme without them can add them (with their readability pairs, described in `DESIGN.md` section 3 and logged in the change log):

- `brand`: the brand's own colour for bands, panels, illustration fills and a highlighted stat. Never for buttons or links: those stay `action-primary`, so people can still tell what they can press. The two are one family: unless the brief names a separate button colour, `action-primary` is the brand's own hue (a deeper shade of it when the brand itself is too light for white text) or a close neighbour of it, never an unrelated one. A clear yellow or amber brand is the exception here too: its buttons are that same yellow with near-black `on-action-primary` text, never a rust, orange or brown stand-in, and the band behind the hero turns `brand-soft` or `surface` so the yellow button stands out on it. Every filled button on every screen is that one `action-primary`: never a dark button in the hero, an orange one in a card and a rust one at the foot. A blue button beside an orange brand and logo reads as two colour systems at a glance, and judges call it a clash.
- `on-brand`: text and icons on a `brand` fill. For an orange, red, green or blue, pick `brand` deep enough that `on-brand` can be white or near-white at 4.5:1 or more (a burnt orange, not a tangerine; a forest green, not a lime): near-black text on a bright orange or green band looks muddy at a glance, even when it passes. A yellow or amber is the exception: when the brief says sunny, golden or bright, keep `brand` a clear warm yellow with near-black `on-brand` text, as a rust or mustard stand-in reads muted and heavier than the brief asks for.
- `brand-soft`: a quiet tint for icon tiles, highlighted rows and the ground behind an illustration.

On a `brand` band, the main button is drawn in `on-brand` with `brand` text (or `surface` with `text-primary`), so it stays the strongest thing on the band; a second action is an outline or a text link. In dark mode the band is deeper and smaller, as large saturated areas are in [visual craft](visual-craft.md).

## Building a brand moment

- **Web welcome or landing page:** a `brand` band (or a `brand-soft` one for gentler briefs) holding a `display` headline of two short lines, one line on what the app does for them, the main action and a quiet second one, then proof: a row of facts ("7 runs a week · 3–16 km · free to join"), the faces of people who use it, or a preview of the app's main screen with realistic content. Below the band, the page can calm down.
- **The first screen is the whole pitch:** headline, one line, the main action and the brand moment all visible without scrolling, on a phone and a desktop.
- **How much follows depends on the product.** A tool people weigh up before they pay for it (invoicing, a business or money app) tells the whole story below the first screen, in this order: what it does (three rows), how it works (three numbered steps), proof (a short quote with a name, or a few real numbers), a closing band with the main action again, and a footer. A simple everyday app (plants, habits) stays focused: one section showing a sample of its real content as proof (a plant's care card), a closing line with the main action again, and the footer. A club or group on a desktop shows a little more, each part as matching cards: this week's runs, how to join in three numbered steps, its kinds (pace groups or levels, each with its colour), then a closing band with the main action again; a long page spreads its attention thin, and a hero followed by only three feature rows looks sparse. A neighbourhood or community service (repairs, requests, bookings) sits between the two: after the hero, how it works in three numbered steps, then a short list of recent requests with their status chips ("Leaking tap · Fixed"), then the closing line; its main action also sits in the header. On a desktop the hero's drawing sits beside the headline at the same height, never floating loose with empty space around it. Either way, never the same card grid four times. On a web page, at every width, the proof right under the hero is the next real thing people come for, as one card with its own action (for a personal app whose content is the person's own, a phone shows instead the one-screen welcome of a drawing, the headline, one line, the main action and three short benefits, with no sample card whose button would compete with the main one): "Next up · Sat 6:30 · Corniche · 5 km · 14 going · I'm going", a plant that needs water today with a Water button; for a catalogue people browse (trails, parks, recipes), three of its real items as cards, each with its key facts (length, time, climb, difficulty) and what the brief's people check before they go (the surface, pram- or wheelchair-friendly, how far away), with any closure notice on its card. On a desktop a numbered "How to start" row (three steps) follows them. Real items show what the app is for faster than generic feature cards; three short rows of what it does can follow it, then the closing line and footer. It ends there: one short footer line at most (the name or a link, said once), never a closing tagline plus a footer that repeats the brand.
- **A native phone welcome is one screen, never scrolled:** the drawing (about a quarter of the height), the headline, one line, two or three short rows of what the app does, and the main action with the quiet second one pinned at the bottom within thumb reach. Everything fits with room to spare above the actions; no sample card here, and nothing runs past the bottom edge, as a row cut off by the screen's edge looks broken. If it doesn't fit, drop a row or shrink the drawing.
- **The main action is what most people come to do.** For a club, a group or anything people return to every week, that is seeing what's on ("See this week's runs"), filled in `action-primary`; joining or signing up is the quiet second one. For a personal app whose content is the person's own (their plants, habits, money), a newcomer has nothing to see yet: the main action starts it ("Add your first plant"), never one that assumes they already have some ("See today's plants"). For a service people come to ask something of (report a repair, book a slot, send a request), it is that ask ("Report a problem"), filled; browsing what others asked is the quiet second one. Beside or under the headline, one live request card shows how it goes ("Leaking tap · Reported → Fixer on the way → Fixed"), which says what the service does faster than a lone feature card. Two buttons of the same weight, or the same destination offered twice (a button and a card), read as indecision.
- **The headline can accent one word:** in the `brand` colour, in italic when the headline is set in a serif, or with a `brand-soft` marker behind it. One word, not the whole line.
- **A calm brief's hero is light.** For calm, fresh or quiet briefs the welcome's band is `brand-soft` or `surface` with the brand in the drawing and the button, never a dark or deep-coloured band that feels heavy; the drawing is shown whole, never covered by a card.
- **Phone welcome on the web:** a small illustration (no more than a quarter of the screen, or beside the headline), the headline, one line, then the main action straight under that line (and the quiet second one under it), then the next-up card, then what the app does in three rows. The actions follow the text, never a screen away from it. A native app's welcome is the one-screen layout above.
- **Sign-in:** on a desktop, a `brand` panel beside the form holding a member's quote in large type, an illustration or a preview of the product; any drawing on a sign-in shows the activity itself at a glance (a runner mid-stride, a plant in a pot, a suitcase), never an abstract shape that has to be explained; on a phone it stays small (no taller than a fifth of the screen) so the form starts high; on a phone, a small drawn illustration above the heading for friendly, warm or playful briefs (the app's mark alone is for quiet ones), never both stacked, aligned the same way as the heading under it (all centred or all at the start). On a desktop, for energetic, playful or bold briefs the panel runs the window's full height on one side, its drawing and quote centred in it, and the form sits centred in the other half as the clear focus. For a business or money tool (invoices, accounts, admin), whatever its feel, the panel shows a calm preview of the product's own work (a few invoices marked paid and late, this month's total), on a dark or `brand-soft` panel. When the audience includes people over 60 (residents, members of every age), one centred card instead (about 440 px wide, the drawing or mark at its top) with roomy type: `title` heading, `body` labels and help text, never `caption`. On a desktop, the page keeps its header with the mark, and under the form a short "Why sign in?" list (two or three benefits, each with an icon) fills the column; a phone leaves it out. Either way: never two small boxed cards floating on an empty background, nor a short pair at the top over an empty bottom half, and the form's edges line up with the header's content edges, never sticking out past them. On a phone, the form is one tidy group, in a `surface` card or straight on the page at full width (never a narrow card inset far from the edges): the drawing or mark at the top, centred or spanning the content width (never a half-width picture at one side), then a large heading at the start, its line, full-width fields and a full-width main button straight under them, and the new-member note, starting in the top third, with no large gap above and none between the field and its button; the sign-in button is never pinned to the bottom away from its field. Everything on it shares one alignment (all at the start, or all centred), and its top bar keeps the back arrow at the start and the language switch at the end. Each message is said once: "your first link creates your account" appears in one place, never under the field and again in a note. Under the form come the new-member note and a quiet way out ("Just browsing? See this week's runs"), then a short footer (the language switch, help) anchoring the bottom, so no large empty area is left under the form. A card inset within the phone's margins squeezes the fields and leaves a blank half below it. Public pages show no signed-in chrome: no avatar, no tab bar.
- **What the app does:** three short rows, each with an icon tile on `brand-soft`, read faster than bare icons or text alone (and aren't the identical card grid from [anti-patterns](anti-patterns.md)).
- **Kinds of content:** when items come in kinds people choose between (a pace, a category, a status), give each kind its own small mark (an icon or a dot in its colour) and its name in words, the same way everywhere. Keep the badges themselves one family: soft tints (`brand-soft`, `surface-raised`) with dark text. A saturated fill is for what needs attention now (late, cancelled, full); three or four saturated colours in one list look scattered.

## Illustration

A simple drawn illustration does more for character than any colour choice. Draw it, don't download it:

- A few geometric shapes in role colours only (`brand`, `brand-soft`, `surface`, `text-primary` and the status colours), so it follows dark mode and `motif check` passes. A subject from the app's world: a plant in a pot, a route line under a sun, a stack of invoices, a ticket.
- A small scene beats a lone object: a soft `brand-soft` shape behind, the subject, and two or three supporting pieces (a smaller plant, a sun, floating icon tiles). A single shape in the middle of a band reads as clip art.
- Draw it with care, as people judge the whole screen by it: every shape is recognisable (a circle is a sun with a soft halo, never a stray dot; a person is a head and shoulders or a whole figure, never a lone blob), everything stands on something (a car on its road, a rider at the stop, never floating), the drawing spans the content's width with its edges lined up with the text under it, the colours stay in one family with one or two cheerful accents (the brand's greens or blues and `brand-soft`, plus a light warm accent such as a soft yellow sun or pink cheeks, never `text-primary` black for an object; a dark text-safe shade like light mode's `warning` reads as mud in a drawing, so take a light tint, or leave the sun out),
  and the main object is filled, not outlined: a terracotta pot, a peach sun, a lavender pot beside it. Warm and soft fills come from mixing a role with `surface`: on the web `color-mix(in srgb, var(--motif-color-warning) 55%, var(--motif-color-surface))` for terracotta and peach, `info` the same way for a soft blue or lavender; natively, the same mix computed from the theme's roles. Mixed from roles, they follow dark mode. A pale object with a dark outline looks stiff next to filled shapes. small details carry the charm (cheeks, a highlight on a leaf, a soft shadow under the pot), and pieces don't overlap awkwardly or get clipped by the frame.
- A small floating card from the app itself ("Monty is thirsty · Water today") next to the drawing ties it to what the app does.
- Look at the drawing on its own in the screenshot before you finish. If it looks rough next to the type, simplify it rather than ship it.
- For playful briefs, a face makes it a mascot (two dots and a smile on the pot).
- One illustration per screen, sized to the moment: about a third of a phone's welcome screen, smaller in an empty state; on working screens only the small item thumbnails described above.
- A drawing never stops at a hard edge partway across the screen: it sits inside a shape (a circle, a soft blob, a rounded card) or runs off the screen's edge.
- It's decoration: hidden from screen readers (`aria-hidden="true"` on the web, excluded from accessibility on native platforms).
- Each stack draws it with its own tools: inline `<svg>` with `fill="var(--motif-color-brand)"` or `currentColor` on the web; `Canvas` in Compose; `CustomPaint` in Flutter; `Shape` and `Path` in SwiftUI; `react-native-svg` in Expo (ask before adding it) or composed views. Mockups use an inline `<svg>` inside the preview kit's `.mk-art`.
- Never emoji in its place (see [emoji as UI](anti-patterns.md#emoji-as-ui)).

## Details that read as care

- **Headlines break well.** No single word alone on the first or last line of a headline, the line under it, or a quote ("A" alone above "neighbour who fixes it" reads as a mistake): `text-wrap: balance` on the web, and shorter copy where a platform can't balance lines.
- **The second button stays quiet.** A second action on `surface` with a `border`, or a text button. Two saturated buttons side by side make people stop and compare.
- **Bilingual apps show their language switch** on public screens and in the main screen's header (at the top end), not only in settings. It is a real control: an outlined pill at least 32 points tall with the other language's own name ("العربية", "English") at `label` size in `text-primary`, centred on the same row as the back button or the mark, never a small coloured text link that floats out of line.
- **Only the headline is in the display face.** The titles of the rows under it (what the app does, a feature) are `heading` in the text family; a second set of large serif titles competes with the headline.
- **Real-looking content.** Names, initials avatars, counts, exact times and places. A list of people going reads warmer than "12 attendees".
- **A desktop is not a stretched phone.** Use the width: a panel beside the form, a preview beside the headline, two columns for a list and its detail. A sidebar earns its width with real navigation; with only two or three places to go, use a top bar instead. Columns side by side start at the same top line. A list row is never wider than about 720px: a wider list becomes a grid of cards or gains a side column, so rows don't stretch across empty space.
- **Playful and energetic briefs feel tactile.** The main button is generous (at least 52px tall on a phone, a heavier label) with a darker edge of 3–4px underneath (on the web, a `box-shadow` of `color-mix()` between `action-primary` and `text-primary`; natively, the same mix computed from the theme's roles) that flattens when pressed. Fields carry a leading icon (mail, lock). Field labels and row titles take the heavier weight (semibold); light labels look unfinished next to a chunky button. On a phone, the sign-in form sits in a `surface` card under the drawing and heading. Quiet briefs keep flat, plain buttons and fields.

## Slips that undo it

People comparing two screens notice small slips before they notice style, and one slip can lose a screen that is better in every other way. Check each first-impression and home screen for these:

- **Every label sits the same distance above its field.** A "Forgot password?" link in the password label's row must not make that row taller (see [sign-in](ux-patterns/sign-in.md)).
- **One focus mark.** A focused field changes its border to `focus` or gets a ring flush against it, never a ring with a gap outside a border that still shows: that reads as a double border. Screenshots often catch the first field focused, so check how it looks.
- **Groups, then gaps.** On a short screen like sign-in, things that belong together sit close (the mark, heading and line; each label and field), the gaps between groups are larger, and fields never drift apart to fill the screen. If a phone screen ends with half of it empty, its top is missing something (the illustration or mark the brief asks for), not its gaps. A short phone screen like sign-in starts its group near the top third, under the drawing or mark, and leaves the rest below; a group floated to the middle with a wide band of nothing above it looks as unfinished as one left at the very top.
- **An empty field looks empty.** Placeholder text is `text-secondary` at about half opacity, clearly lighter than what people type, and plainly an example ("e.g. name@uni.edu"); a dark placeholder or a realistic full address reads as one already filled in.
- **Nothing is focused on load.** A sign-in doesn't autofocus its first field: on a phone it throws up the keyboard, and in a screenshot a highlighted empty field looks like an error.
- **Inline text matches.** A question and its link ("New to Ledgerly? Create an account"), or a value and its unit, share one size and one baseline.
- **Item titles are never smaller than their descriptions.** In a feature row or a list item the title is `heading` (or `body` in a heavier weight) and the description is `body` or `caption` in `text-secondary`, never larger than the title above it.
- **Counts match what's shown.** "To do today (3)" lists three items, or ends with "Show all".
- **Rows line up.** Titles start at the same place in every row: a leading badge whose width changes with its word pushes them out of line, so status badges go at the end of the row (or the leading slot gets one fixed width). On a phone, titles wrap to a second line instead of ending in "…"; shorten the copy if they're long.
- **Each action appears once.** "Sign in" in the header and again beside the main action is one too many; the hero's second action is something else ("See how it works") or nothing.
- **The main action clearly outweighs the second one,** by fill as well as position.
- **A button's label is never small.** A large button's label is `body` size or bigger in a heavier weight, never `label` or `caption` size: a small label in a big button reads as faint, and in a shrunk screenshot it can look like dark text on the fill.
- **Filters sit on one line.** A list's tabs, chips and sort control fit one row at every width: on a desktop, one row with the sort at its end, never one chip wrapped alone onto a second line; on a phone, the row fits the screen's width with nothing cut off by its edge (a chip sliced in half at the edge reads as a bug). Too many to fit means fewer of them, or the rest behind one "Filters" button. A catalogue people search (trails, recipes, places) puts a search field first, then its filters in rows by what they filter (kind in one, difficulty in the next).
- **Facts people compare are figures.** When items are weighed up by their numbers (a trail's length, time and climb; a room's price and size), each card shows them as a short row of figures, the number at `body` size or larger with its label under it ("5.2 km" over "Length"), never squeezed into one line of small grey text. Kinds (difficulty, surface) get their colour-coded chip: each level its own colour (easy green, moderate amber, hard red, the same colours in the filters), never every tag in one colour where difficulty gets lost among the rest. A card shows at most one row of chips, and cards in a grid share one height and one structure: the kind chip, a full-width title, the row of figures, the action. Each line has a fixed place: the title and the place each hold one line (the mock names are short enough to fit; an ellipsis on every card reads as cramped), so the figures, chips and bookmark sit at the same height on every card in the row; one card whose title wraps pushes its figures out of line and the whole row looks uneven. A notice about one item (closed, sold out, delayed) sits on that item's card in a short sentence, not as a tiny tag, coloured by how serious it is (`warning` for a partial closure, `danger` for closed). A per-row extra (Save) is an icon button, not a full button repeated down the list.
- **A working list on a desktop is a full screen.** Its header holds a large title with the main action beside it ("Report a problem"), then tabs for its states (Open, In progress, Fixed) and a search field; each item is a card that answers the questions people come with at a glance (its status chip, how urgent or risky, who is on it and when). Its status is colour-coded (a coloured edge or badge in the status's role, with its word), and the action goes where it's needed: a strong "I can fix this" on the requests waiting for someone, a quiet "View" on the rest; the same faint button on every card makes them all look alike. A short list in a narrow column with the rest of the screen empty reads as unfinished.
- **A desktop section fills its width.** A list or a row of cards spans the content width (each request a full-width row with its status chip, who's on it and when), or sits beside a second column of matching height; never a narrow list stranded at one side of an empty area. A single card is the exception: it never stretches across the page, where its few lines leave a long empty row. On a desktop the next-up card sits in the hero beside the headline, in place of the drawing or in its own column next to it, never over it: a card that crops the drawing looks like a layout bug.
- **Columns side by side end together.** A card beside a taller column stretches to the same height (its action pinned to its foot), or the cards sit in one row of equal cards instead; a short card beside a tall stack leaves a hole that looks unfinished.
- **Every control is styled.** A sort or filter control matches the chips beside it (a styled select or segmented control with the active option filled), never the browser's native dropdown; no stray buttons with no clear job (a lone bookmark beside the search); and a card's own buttons never sit over the subject of its drawing.
- **A list has no holes.** A list grouped by day or kind shows only the groups with something in them, one after another at the same spacing; a blank block between Saturday and Sunday looks like something failed to load.
- **Progress never starts empty.** The mock data shows some progress ("2 of 5 done" with the bar part-filled), or the summary shows a count instead of a bar; an empty bar looks broken.
- **A desktop welcome reads from across the room.** Its section headings are `title`, the text under them `body` (never `label` or `caption`), and the line under the headline is larger than body text: a page of uniformly small text looks flat beside one with a clear type scale.
- **Section headings stand clear of the text.** A section's heading is `title` or `heading`, a clear step above `body`, never body size in bold: headings barely bigger than the text under them flatten the page.
- **A stat's label is readable.** Under a big number the label is `body` or `label` size, never `caption`: "runs a week" in tiny grey under "7" reads as an afterthought from across the room.
- **The hero's headline owns its band.** On a desktop the headline is `display` and fills about half the band's width; a small heading floating on a large coloured band looks lost.
- **Drawings show whole.** A hero or panel drawing fits inside its band with room around it, never cut off by the band's edge or the window's; a clipped sun or hill looks like a layout bug.
- **Small grey text is rare.** A row's second line is `label` or `body` in `text-secondary`, not `caption`; `caption` is for a timestamp or a count. A screen where most of the text is small and grey looks unfinished.
- **Repeated actions look alike and stay put.** Every row's action sits in the same place (the end of the row) at the same width, whatever it says: "Join", "Going ✓" or "Full" share one shape, with the state in its words and colour, never a paler button that looks disabled, one that moves below the text in some rows, or one cut off at the screen's edge: the row's text wraps or shortens, the button keeps its width, and nothing ("3 spots left") ever sits under or behind the button. A row's quieter link ("Can't make it") lines up with the row's text. Every row's action uses the same one style on a screen (the same filled pill on each card), never tinted on some, outlined on others and shadowed on a third. Buttons people should press look pressable: the row's action is filled in `action-primary` or outlined in it, never a `brand-soft` or `action-secondary` fill, which reads as disabled next to a strong button elsewhere.
- **Only what can be pressed looks pressable.** A white or outlined pill reads as a button. Facts and features that do nothing are rows with an icon tile, or plain tinted text, never a row of white pills.
- **A control beside a heading sits on its line.** A filter or sort next to a section heading shares the heading's centre line, with its label inside the control ("Pace: All") or as its accessible name, never a label floating above it.
- **Phone rows leave the title room.** If a row's time column and its action leave the title less than half the width, titles wrap word by word: move the time into the row's first line ("Today · 18:30") or the action below the text, so a title wraps at most once.
- **The header lines up with the page.** On a desktop, the header's mark and links sit in the same centred column as the content under them (the same max width and side padding), never pinned to the window's edges above a narrower column.
- **Every action looks like one, and sign-in has one big button.** A sign-in has one button: the filled main one. The way in without an account is one quiet text link under it ("Just looking? See this week's runs"), in `action-primary` with no fill or border; no "or" divider, no second outlined button, and no row of three or four coloured links competing with the main button. "Forgot password" or "Email me a link" sits small under its field.
- **The sign-in page doesn't offer to sign in again.** Its header carries the mark and a way back, never a "Sign in" button beside the form that already is one.
- **The mark appears once per screen.** A desktop sign-in with a `brand` panel puts the logo on the panel or above the form, not both.
- **One heading per thing.** A page title and a section title right under it that say the same ("This week" over "This week's runs") are one too many: drop one.
- **Avatars are never blank.** Every person shows initials on a tinted circle; a dashed or empty placeholder circle reads as missing data.
- **Numbers use the text face's figures.** Times and counts use the body font with `tabular-nums`, never a monospace or a slashed zero ("1Ø:3Ø" looks like code).
- **Overlapping avatars keep every initial whole.** They overlap by at most a quarter of their width, each with a ring in the surface colour, and the initials stay centred and uncut.
- **Short labels don't break.** Chips, badges and short facts ("Living room", "12 going") never wrap mid-phrase: shorten them, or let the whole chip move to the next line.
- **Home leads with one thing.** Today's focus first (the next run, today's plants, the amount owed), then a few rows and "See all". On a phone that is the focus, a quick way to pick the day or kind, and the list; extra sections (regulars, monthly stats) belong on their own screens or a desktop's side column. The item in the focus card isn't repeated in the list under it.
- **A schedule reads by day and time.** Things that happen at a time (runs, classes, bookings) are grouped under day headings ("Today", "Tomorrow", "Sat 2 Nov"), and every card has one compact layout: the time in a column at the start ("06:30" in `heading`), then the title on its own line and the place, distance and pace on the next, at `body` size and shown in full, and the action at the end. Room comes first: a card that cuts every place name off ("Elm Park track…") or shrinks its text to fit reads as cramped. Every card is the same height and shape whatever its details, at phone and desktop widths alike; cards that differ in size or wrap their details differently read as unfinished. A phone's first screen is not a dense list of everything, and a desktop's is not one narrow column beside a large empty area.
- **No stray scaffolding.** No "Demo: name / password" hints, "Lorem", placeholder routes or debug labels on screen; mock sign-in details go in the README.
- **Backgrounds run to the end.** A sidebar's colour reaches the bottom of a long page (let the layout's grid stretch it, with the navigation inside it sticky), and a gradient covers the whole page instead of stopping in a band.

## Before you finish

1. The five-second test passes on the welcome, sign-in and home screens, on a phone and a desktop.
2. Each first-impression screen has its brand moment, as loud as the brief asks.
3. None of the slips above, on any of those screens: look at each screenshot once just for them.
4. Working screens still pass the [visual craft](visual-craft.md) checklist: the brand didn't spill into buttons, links or long lists.
5. Illustrations use role colours only, look right in dark mode, and are hidden from screen readers.
