# Character

A screen can follow every rule in the design guide and still look as if nobody cared about it: tidy, correct and forgettable. People decide in the first few seconds whether an app feels like what it is, so the brief's personality has to show at a glance. This file says where the brand gets to be loud, how loud, and how to build those moments from the guide's roles. The other knowledge files say what to avoid; this one says what to add.

## The five-second test

Look at a screenshot of the welcome screen for five seconds, then write down three words for how it feels. Compare them with the personality words in `DESIGN.md` section 1.

- If your words match ("energetic, bold, friendly" for a running club that asked for energetic and friendly), the screen has character.
- If your words are "clean, tidy, plain" or "calm, muted" and the brief asked for something else, the screen isn't finished, however correct it is.
- **No empty areas.** An empty band of the screen (under the header at the top, above the tab bar or footer at the bottom, beside a short column on a desktop) fails the screen: it reads as unfinished, not calm. Fill it with the user's own content, a key figure, an illustration or a closing call to action, never with filler. When the developer gave the screen's content and it is short, bring it together instead: one group centred in the screen on a first-impression screen, the list across the width on a working one, and no large drawing or side panel added just to use up room.
- Every one of the brief's words counts, not just the loudest. "Playful, fresh, friendly" for a brief of "playful, fresh, gentle, reassuring" misses two of them. When you compare directions, the one that misses the fewest wins; a full saturated band reads as bold or energetic, never as gentle or calm.

Run it on the welcome or landing screen, sign-in and home, on a phone and (for web apps) a desktop.

## Turn the dial to the brief

How loud the brand gets follows the personality words, not a house style. The brief's words set how loud, not whether: every brief, the calmest included, gets crafted moments on its first-impression and home screens.

| The brief says | Brand moments |
|---|---|
| calm, precise, quiet, professional, premium | A pairing whose headings are a serif (the Editorial style's, or a serif display with a quiet sans for text): a sans-only pairing reads generic here. A light page with one refined moment per first-impression screen: a well-set `display` headline (in the serif, with one word accented), a realistic preview of the product on a raised card, generous space, a short label above the headline. A deep `brand` area at most once: the sign-in panel or the closing band of a landing page, never a heavy block at the top. Working screens stay calm but crafted (the floor below), never plain. |
| friendly, gentle, warm, reassuring | Soft `brand-soft` tints, a friendly illustration on the welcome screen and empty states, rounded icon tiles, warm copy. |
| playful and gentle together (fun words with soft ones: gentle, fresh, reassuring, cosy) | A light page with a soft `brand-soft` wash, a carefully drawn illustrated scene (a mascot is welcome) beside or above the headline, one headline word in `brand`, what the app does as three short rows with icon tiles, rounded generous buttons. No saturated full-width band: the colour lives in the drawing and the accents. |
| energetic, bold, playful, fun, adventurous | A full `brand` band on the welcome screen, a big `display` headline in the heaviest weight the font has, an illustration or mascot, colour-coded content, proof in numbers and faces, lively motion. |

A quiet brief still gets its one moment, and a loud brief still keeps its working screens easy to read. When the words mix rows, the gentler word sets the ground and the louder one shows in the details.

## Where the brand is loud

- **First impressions:** the welcome or landing page, onboarding, sign-in, empty states and success moments. The brand lives here.
- **Working screens:** lists, forms, settings and dashboards. The [visual craft](visual-craft.md) rules hold, and character shows in the details ([working screens](#working-screens) below).

## Full home and landing screens

- **Show the user's own picture, not one number.** Home and landing show the key figures and a breakdown of them: what is owed and how old it is, this week's runs and who is going, the plants due today and the next one after. Each element answers "what's happening" or "what should I do next"; one that answers neither is decoration and goes.
- **Apps without numbers fill with their own content:** the next meetup, the upcoming trip, saved routes, the people involved, counts and progress. Fullness means more of that, never invented charts.
- **Fuller isn't busier.** Group what belongs together in cards, keep one primary action, and on a desktop set the cards side by side in a real desktop layout ([visual craft](visual-craft.md)).

## The brand roles

Every starting style has three optional colour roles for this; a theme without them can add them (with their readability pairs, described in `DESIGN.md` section 3 and logged in the change log):

- `brand`: the brand's own colour for bands, panels, illustration fills and a highlighted stat. Never for buttons or links: those stay `action-primary`, so people can still tell what they can press. The two are one family: unless the brief names a separate button colour, `action-primary` is the brand's own hue (a deeper shade of it when the brand itself is too light for white text) or a close neighbour of it, never an unrelated one. A clear yellow or amber brand is the exception here too: its buttons are that same yellow with near-black `on-action-primary` text, never a rust, orange or brown stand-in, and the ground right behind them is `surface` (the hero's band turns `surface`, or the button sits on a `surface` card): on `brand`, or on `brand-soft`, which is only a paler yellow, a yellow button blends into its own hue. Every filled button on every screen is that one `action-primary` (one colour; how many are filled is a matter of [weight](#slips-that-undo-it)): never a dark button in the hero, an orange one in a card and a rust one at the foot. A blue button beside an orange brand and logo reads as two colour systems at a glance, and judges call it a clash.
- `on-brand`: text and icons on a `brand` fill. For an orange, red, green or blue, pick `brand` deep enough that `on-brand` can be white or near-white at 4.5:1 or more (a burnt orange, not a tangerine; a forest green, not a lime): near-black text on a bright orange or green band looks muddy at a glance, even when it passes. A yellow or amber is the exception: when the brief says sunny, golden or bright, keep `brand` a clear warm yellow with near-black `on-brand` text, as a rust or mustard stand-in reads muted and heavier than the brief asks for.
- `brand-soft`: a quiet tint for icon tiles, highlighted rows and the ground behind an illustration.

On a `brand` band, the main button is drawn in `on-brand` with `brand` text (or `surface` with `text-primary`), so it stays the strongest thing on the band; a second action is an outline or a text link. In dark mode the band is deeper and smaller, as large saturated areas are in [visual craft](visual-craft.md).

## First-impression recipes

Every first-impression screen is built from the same few parts, sized to the brief's dial: the `hero` headline (the one largest line on the screen); the brand moment (a `brand` or `brand-soft` band or panel, or the drawing); `accent` and `accent-2` for small touches within the area cap in [visual craft](visual-craft.md) (a marker behind one word, a dot, a second glow); at most one depth piece ([tonal gradient](../library/depth/tonal-gradient.md), [soft glow](../library/depth/soft-glow.md) or [pattern](../library/depth/pattern.md)); and the style's signature on its buttons, cards and badges where the style has it on by default (Playful, Bold and Dark tech; see each style's file under `library/accents/`). The [library](../library/README.md) has a piece for each screen below. When the developer said what a screen shows, that content is the whole of it: the recipe's own additions below (a next-up card, a proof row, a preview or sample card, a floating card, a quote) are left out, and the brand moment is the drawing, the band and the type.

- **Web welcome or landing page** ([landing hero](../library/screens/landing-hero.md)): a `brand` band (or a `brand-soft` one for gentler briefs) holding a `hero` headline of two short lines, one line on what the app does for them, the main action and a quiet second one, then proof: a row of facts ("7 runs a week · 3–16 km · free to join"), the faces of people who use it, or a preview of the app's main screen with realistic content. A tonal gradient on the band or a soft glow behind the drawing gives it depth. Below the band, the page can calm down.
- **The first screen is the whole pitch:** headline, one line, the main action and the brand moment all visible without scrolling, on a phone and a desktop. The brand moment serves that pitch and gives way to it: when room is short, the drawing shrinks or moves beside the headline before the message or the main action moves down. A short welcome (a headline, one line, a few points and the action, with no more to tell) is one group. On a desktop its points sit under the action in the headline's own column, beside a drawing at least as tall as that column, and that column and the drawing are centred together in the first screen: one group in the middle of the window, with no separate strip of points under it and nothing left alone at the window's top or foot. On a phone the points come straight after the actions as compact rows (an icon tile and a phrase each, not a card apiece) on a ground of their own: one `surface` card inside the page's margins, one section's gap under the sign-in line. At both sizes the points are held together as one block (that card, or on a desktop a hairline over them) and sit close under the actions: loose rows left under the sign-in link, or a band to the screen's edges with rules across it, read as a strip added afterwards. The whole welcome is about one screen tall.
- **How much follows depends on the product.** A tool people weigh up before they pay for it (invoicing, a business or money app) tells the whole story below the first screen, in this order: what it does (three rows), how it works (three numbered steps), proof (a short quote with a name, or a few real numbers), a closing band with the main action again, and a footer. A simple everyday app (plants, habits) stays focused: one section showing a sample of its real content as proof (a plant's care card), a closing line with the main action again, and the footer. A club or group on a desktop shows a little more, each part as matching cards: this week's runs, how to join in three numbered steps, its kinds (pace groups or levels, each with its colour), then a closing band with the main action again; a long page spreads its attention thin, and a hero followed by only three feature rows looks sparse. A neighbourhood or community service (repairs, requests, bookings) sits between the two: after the hero, how it works in three numbered steps, then a short list of recent requests with their status chips ("Leaking tap · Fixed"), then the closing line; its main action also sits in the header. On a desktop the hero's drawing sits beside the headline at the same height, filling its column rather than floating loose with empty space around it. Either way, vary the sections rather than repeating one card grid four times. On a web page, at every width, the proof right under the hero is the next real thing people come for, as one card with its own action (for a personal app whose content is the person's own, a phone shows instead the one-screen welcome of a drawing, the headline, one line, the main action and three short benefits, with no sample card whose button would compete with the main one): "Next up · Sat 6:30 · Corniche · 5 km · 14 going · I'm going"; for a personal app, a sample of what it keeps for you (a plant's care card: its drawing, its name, "water every 7 days", "next: Sat") with no button of its own, since a visitor has no plants yet and "Water" or "Mark as watered" on a plant that isn't theirs makes no sense; for a catalogue people browse (trails, parks, recipes), three of its real items as cards, each with its key facts (length, time, climb, difficulty) and what the brief's people check before they go (the surface, pram- or wheelchair-friendly, how far away), with any closure notice on its card. On a desktop a numbered "How to start" row (three steps) follows them. Real items show what the app is for faster than generic feature cards; three short rows of what it does can follow it, then the closing line and footer. It ends there: one short footer line at most (the name or a link, said once), not a closing tagline plus a footer that repeats the brand.
- **A native phone welcome fits one screen without scrolling** ([welcome](../library/screens/welcome.md)): the drawing (about a quarter of the height), the headline, one line, two or three short rows of what the app does, and the main action with the quiet second one pinned at the bottom within thumb reach. Everything fits with room to spare above the actions; no sample card here, and nothing runs past the bottom edge, as a row cut off by the screen's edge looks broken. If it doesn't fit, drop a row or shrink the drawing.
- **The main action is what most people come to do.** For a club, a group or anything people return to every week, that is seeing what's on ("See this week's runs"), filled in `action-primary`; joining or signing up is the quiet second one. For a personal app whose content is the person's own (their plants, habits, money), a newcomer has nothing to see yet: the main action starts it ("Add your first plant"), not one that assumes they already have some ("See today's plants"). For a service people come to ask something of (report a repair, book a slot, send a request), it is that ask ("Report a problem"), filled; browsing what others asked is the quiet second one. Beside or under the headline, one live request card shows how it goes ("Leaking tap · Reported → Fixer on the way → Fixed"), which says what the service does faster than a lone feature card. Two buttons of the same weight, or the same destination offered twice (a button and a card), read as indecision.
- **The headline can accent one word:** in the `brand` colour, in italic when the headline is set in a serif, or with an `accent` or `brand-soft` marker behind it. One word, not the whole line.
- **A calm brief's hero is light.** For calm, fresh or quiet briefs the welcome's band is `brand-soft` or `surface` with the brand in the drawing and the button, rather than a dark or deep-coloured band that feels heavy; the drawing shows whole, uncovered by any card. A pattern is its depth piece, if it has one.
- **Phone welcome on the web:** a small illustration (no more than a quarter of the screen, or beside the headline), the headline, one line, then the main action straight under that line (and the quiet second one under it), then the next-up card, then what the app does in three rows. The actions follow the text directly, on the same screen. A short welcome with only its points to show keeps to about one screen: the drawing is the first thing to shrink, and the rows follow the actions after one section's gap, not a band of space, together in one `surface` card. A native app's welcome is the one-screen layout above.
- **Sign-in** ([sign-in](../library/screens/sign-in.md)): on a desktop, a rich `brand` panel (a layered preview of the product's own cards, or a drawing of the activity itself, with a member's quote; with given content, the drawing alone) beside a crafted form with a `hero` headline; on a phone, one column with a small drawing or mark at the top and the form starting in the top third. The form offers only the sign-in methods the app really has. A business or money tool's panel previews the product's normal work (paid, sent, on track), so it stays calm beside the form. Each message is said once, and public pages show no signed-in chrome: no avatar, no tab bar, and no action on content the visitor doesn't have yet ("Mark as watered", "Edit"); an action that joins something ("I'm going") may stay, and leads to sign-in.
- **What the app does:** three short rows, each with an icon tile on `brand-soft`, read faster than bare icons or text alone (and aren't the identical card grid from [anti-patterns](anti-patterns.md)).
- **Kinds of content:** when items come in kinds people choose between (a pace, a category, a status), give each kind its own small mark (an icon or a dot in its colour) and its name in words, the same way everywhere. Keep the badges themselves one family: soft tints (`brand-soft`, `surface-raised`) with dark text. A saturated fill is for what needs attention now (late, nearly full, about to close), and an item that is off is muted, not loud ([working screens](#working-screens)); three or four saturated colours in one list look scattered.

## Illustration

A simple drawn illustration does more for character than any colour choice. Draw it, don't download it:

- A few geometric shapes in role colours only (`brand`, `brand-soft`, `surface`, `text-primary` and the status colours), so it follows dark mode and `motif check` passes. A subject from the app's world: a plant in a pot, a route line under a sun, a stack of invoices, a ticket.
- A small scene beats a lone object: a soft `brand-soft` shape behind, the subject, and two or three supporting pieces (a smaller plant, a sun, floating icon tiles). A single shape in the middle of a band reads as clip art.
- Draw it with care, as people judge the whole screen by it: every shape is recognisable (a circle is a sun with a soft halo, never a stray dot; a person is a head and shoulders or a whole figure, never a lone blob), everything stands on something (a car on its road, a rider at the stop, never floating), the drawing spans the content's width with its edges lined up with the text under it, the colours stay in one family with one or two cheerful accents (the brand's greens or blues and `brand-soft`, plus a light warm accent such as a soft yellow sun or pink cheeks, never `text-primary` black for an object; a dark text-safe shade like light mode's `warning` reads as mud in a drawing, so take a light tint, or leave the sun out),
  and the main object is filled, not outlined: a terracotta pot, a peach sun, a lavender pot beside it. Warm and soft fills come from mixing a role with `surface`: on the web `color-mix(in srgb, var(--motif-color-warning) 55%, var(--motif-color-surface))` for terracotta and peach, `info` the same way for a soft blue or lavender; natively, the same mix computed from the theme's roles. Mixed from roles, they follow dark mode. A pale object with a dark outline looks stiff next to filled shapes. small details carry the charm (cheeks, a highlight on a leaf, a soft shadow under the pot), and pieces don't overlap awkwardly or get clipped by the frame.
- A small floating card from the app itself ("Monty is thirsty · Water today") next to the drawing ties it to what the app does.
- Look at the drawing on its own in the screenshot before you finish. If it looks rough next to the type, simplify it rather than ship it.
- For playful briefs, a face makes it a mascot (two dots and a smile on the pot).
- One illustration per screen, sized to the moment: about a quarter of a phone's welcome screen, as its recipe says, smaller in an empty state; on working screens only the small item thumbnails described above.
- A drawing never stops at a hard edge partway across the screen: it sits inside a shape (a circle, a soft blob, a rounded card) or runs off the screen's edge.
- It's decoration: hidden from screen readers (`aria-hidden="true"` on the web, excluded from accessibility on native platforms).
- Each stack draws it with its own tools: inline `<svg>` with `fill="var(--motif-color-brand)"` or `currentColor` on the web; `Canvas` in Compose; `CustomPaint` in Flutter; `Shape` and `Path` in SwiftUI; `react-native-svg` in Expo (ask before adding it) or composed views. Mockups use an inline `<svg>` inside the preview kit's `.mk-art`.
- Never emoji in its place (see [emoji as UI](anti-patterns.md#emoji-as-ui)).

## Details that read as care

- **Headlines break well.** No single word alone on the first or last line of a headline, the line under it, or a quote ("A" alone above "neighbour who fixes it" reads as a mistake): `text-wrap: balance` on the web, and shorter copy where a platform can't balance lines.
- **The second button stays quiet.** A second action on `surface` with a `border`, or a text button. Two saturated buttons side by side make people stop and compare.
- **Bilingual apps show their language switch** on public screens and in the main screen's header (at the top end), not only in settings. It is a real control: an outlined pill at least 32 points tall with the other language's own name ("العربية", "English") at `label` size in `text-primary`, centred on the same row as the back button or the mark, never a small coloured text link that floats out of line.
- **Only the headline is in the display face.** The titles of the rows under it (what the app does, a feature) are `heading` in the text family; a second set of large serif titles competes with the headline.
- **Real-looking content.** Names, initials avatars, counts, exact times and places. A list of people going reads warmer than "12 attendees".
- **A desktop is not a stretched phone.** Use the width: a panel beside the form, a preview beside the headline, two columns for a list and its detail. A sidebar earns its width with real navigation; with only two or three places to go, use a top bar instead. Columns side by side start at the same top line. A list row is never wider than about 720px, so rows don't stretch across empty space: a list people read in order (by date, by time, as a queue) stays one centred column of cards about that wide, and a list of things picked in any order (places, products) becomes a grid of cards or gains a side column as tall as the list.
- **Playful and energetic briefs feel tactile.** The main button is generous (at least 52px tall on a phone, a heavier label) with a darker edge of 3–4px underneath (on the web, a `box-shadow` of `color-mix()` between `action-primary` and `text-primary`; natively, the same mix computed from the theme's roles) that flattens when pressed. Fields carry a leading icon (mail, lock). Field labels and row titles take the heavier weight (semibold); light labels look unfinished next to a chunky button. On a phone, the sign-in form sits in a `surface` card under the drawing and heading. Quiet briefs keep flat, plain buttons and fields.

## Working screens

Lists, forms, settings and dashboards. Here the [visual craft](visual-craft.md) rules hold: about a tenth accent, one primary action, `action-primary` only for acting. Character shows in the details: the screen's key number in `display`, today's focus card (the next run, the amount owed) in `brand` with `on-brand` text when the brief is energetic, bold, playful or friendly on the web (a pale card there reads as flat); in a native iPhone app it is a `surface` card on the system's grouped background, the brand in its figure, its icon and its button, since a full brand slab on a native screen reads loud, initials avatars of the people involved, tinted category tiles, an illustrated empty state.

For playful, friendly or energetic briefs the dial reaches these screens too, or they read as "clean but plain" beside the welcome screen: each item gets a small drawn thumbnail from the same family as the welcome drawing (the plant's pot with its face, a run's route line), kinds get colour-coded tags, a to-do gets a round check-off circle, buttons feel tactile (below), and on the web the screen's title is set in the `display` face on a header band in `brand`, `brand-soft` or a wash of the brand, kept short on a phone (the title and one line, about a fifth of the screen at most), with the list's main action on a ground it [stands clear of](#slips-that-undo-it). This holds at every width: a desktop home keeps the thumbnails and tags rather than switching to a plain table.

Items that are places or things with a look of their own (a trail, a park, a plant, a trip) get the drawn thumbnail in any brief but a business tool's, calm ones included: a trail list without its route pictures reads as a plain text list. Each thumbnail is that item's own small scene (dunes for a desert trip, a harbour for a coast, a ridge for a mountain trail), never one placeholder shape repeated down the list. A place people travel to or along (a trail, a route, a ride, a trip) also gets a small map on its card: the route as a line from a start dot to an end dot, beside the picture or in a corner of it, so the card shows both what it looks like and where it goes.

A card carries what people decide by (who, when, where, how far, how many seats), each fact once, in labelled rows or a row of figures; more facts in order read as care, the same facts in one dense line read as clutter. The picture never squeezes the text: on a card it is a short banner across the card's top, or a small square (about 64 px) beside the title; on a phone's list, each card keeps its drawn banner across the top, kept short (about 16:5, a quarter of the card at most) so the list stays quick to scroll, then the name on one line with the status badge ("Closed", "Partly closed") at its end, in the same place on every card, then the figures each led by a small icon; a detail that doesn't fit (a surface type) waits for the item's own page rather than being cut off with an ellipsis; a large picture beside a narrow text column makes titles break on lone words and chips spill onto extra lines.

Calm and business briefs (invoicing, money, admin) get the calm-but-crafted floor on their home screen: one deep `brand` card holding the key figure with a small drawn trend line, a progress ring for a goal or a share paid, a segmented bar for parts of a whole (invoices by age: current, 1–30 days, 31–60 days, 60+ days), and status colour only on what needs action ("Overdue · 4 invoices"). The line draws and the segments grow in, at the calm motion level ([motion](motion.md)). These screens stay flat: no depth piece, glow, tilt or gradient backdrop on a chart or a card; depth belongs to first-impression screens ([anti-patterns](anti-patterns.md)).

Check each working screen for these as well:

- **Filters sit on one line.** A list's tabs, chips and sort control fit one row at every width: on a desktop, one row with the sort at its end, never one chip wrapped alone onto a second line; on a phone, the row fits the screen's width with nothing cut off by its edge (a chip sliced in half at the edge reads as a bug). Too many to fit means fewer of them, or the rest behind one "Filters" button. A catalogue people search (trails, recipes, places) puts a search field first, then its filters in rows by what they filter (kind in one, difficulty in the next).
- **Facts people compare are figures.** When items are weighed up by their numbers (a trail's length, time and climb; a room's price and size), each card shows them as a short row of figures, the number at `body` size or larger with its label under it ("5.2 km" over "Length"), never squeezed into one line of small grey text. Status pills are soft tints: the status's colour as text on a pale fill of the same hue (`danger` on a light red tint), never a solid saturated block, which shouts on every row it repeats on; only the one thing that needs action today may be solid. Kinds (difficulty, surface) get their colour-coded chip: each level its own colour (easy green, moderate amber, hard red, the same colours in the filters), never every tag in one colour where difficulty gets lost among the rest. A level written inside a fact (a pace or a grade given as a word beside its number) is still a kind: it is lifted out of the line into its chip. A card shows at most one row of chips, and cards in a grid share one height and one structure: the kind chip, a full-width title, the row of figures, the action. A line only some cards have (a notice, a waiting count) takes the slot of one the others have (the status's), so no card is stretched around a blank gap in its middle. Each line has a fixed place: the title and the place each hold one line (the mock names are short enough to fit; an ellipsis on every card reads as cramped), so the figures, chips and bookmark sit at the same height on every card in the row; one card whose title wraps pushes its figures out of line and the whole row looks uneven. A notice about one item (closed, sold out, delayed) sits on that item's card in a short sentence, not as a tiny tag, coloured by how serious it is (`warning` for a partial closure, `danger` for closed), as text on a pale tint of that colour like the status pills: a solid bar across the card's foot, where its button would be, reads as a button nobody can press. A per-row extra (Save) is an icon button, not a full button repeated down the list.
- **A working list on a desktop is a full screen.** Its header holds a large title with the main action beside it ("Report a problem"), then tabs for its states (Open, In progress, Fixed) and a search field; each item is a card that answers the questions people come with at a glance (its status chip, how urgent or risky, who is on it and when). Its status is colour-coded (a coloured edge or badge in the status's role, with its word). A status told in a sentence (who is handling it, what happens next) is colour-coded the same way: the sentence sits under the title in a pale tint of its status's colour, with that status's icon at its start, in the same box on every card and smaller than the title; a small coloured mark beside plain text is too little to tell the cards apart. The action goes where it's needed: a strong "I can fix this" on the requests waiting for someone, a quiet "View" on the rest; the same faint button on every card makes them all look alike. A short list in a narrow column at one side, with the rest of the screen empty, reads as unfinished. A list in date, time or queue order is one column, centred under that header: each card is stacked, its title and facts above and its status and its action on one line at its foot. In a grid the order zigzags and the last card leaves an empty slot beside it, and a row split into three columns (title, status, button) ends in a wide button around a short word. The list keeps the width: its title, its count and its main action are the header across the top, not a card or a coloured panel at the side, where the room under them stays empty for the length of the list. A side column is for content as tall as the list (its filters, a map, the chosen item's detail), and a drawing or a decorative panel there takes room from the rows.
- **An item's state shows on its whole card.** In a list, three kinds of card look different at a squint, before a badge is read. The person's own item (they are in, they hold a place, it is waiting on them) stands out: a `brand-soft` ground and a 2 px edge in `brand` (`action-secondary` and `action-primary` in a theme without the brand roles). Where the card's button would then sit on a tint of its own hue, as a light action colour does, the ground stays `surface` and the card's tile or leading edge is filled in `brand` instead. An item that is off (called off, closed, gone with no queue) steps back: a flat `background` ground with no shadow, its title struck through in `text-secondary`, its tile or picture greyed, the reason in one short line, and no button; the card is as tall as its content, not stretched to its neighbours' height around a blank gap where the button was. The rest stay plain. A hairline in another colour, or a title a shade paler, is too little to see. State changes a card's ground, edge and title, and leaves its shape and the place of its status alone: the status sits in the same spot on every card, on the card's body (at the end of the title's line, or on the action's line) and not on its picture.
- **A chart says what it shows.** Its title, its axis values ("$0", "$5K", "$10K"; the months), and its total or average beside it; an unlabelled chart reads as decoration.
- **A list has no holes.** A list grouped by day or kind shows only the groups with something in them, one after another at the same spacing; a blank block between Saturday and Sunday looks like something failed to load.
- **Progress never starts empty.** The mock data shows some progress ("2 of 5 done" with the bar part-filled), or the summary shows a count instead of a bar; an empty bar looks broken.
- **Home leads with one thing.** Today's focus first (the next run, today's plants, the amount owed), then a few rows and "See all". A business or money tool's home on a desktop is its full working dashboard, whatever its feel: a row of its key numbers (outstanding, overdue, collected this month, how long payment takes), what needs action with its button ("Remind" on each overdue invoice), what is coming due, a timeline of recent activity, a chart of the money over time (collected per month for the last 12, with the total and the monthly average), and the full list with tabs for its states and a search. A calm brief is calm in its colours and type, not in how little it shows: a big total and two short lists look unfinished beside it.
- **Nothing hides under a bar.** A floating tab bar, a floating add button or a pinned bottom button never covers content: the list ends with room above the bar (SwiftUI `.safeAreaInset(edge: .bottom)`, Compose's `Scaffold` padding, Flutter's `bottomNavigationBar`, padding as tall as the bar on the web), and nothing is drawn below it. A floating button sits over the end of a row, so when each row ends in its own button it would cover one of them wherever the list is scrolled: that action goes in the header or under the list instead. On a phone that is the focus, a quick way to pick the day or kind, and the list; extra sections (regulars, monthly stats) belong on their own screens or a desktop's side column. The item in the focus card isn't repeated in the list under it.
- **A trip or a day reads as a timeline.** The next trip's day is a vertical timeline: each stop with its time, a small icon and a colour for its kind (travel, booking, place), joined by a thin line, so the day's shape shows at a glance. A warm brief takes warm colours throughout (sand, sunset, terracotta), not a cool blue card.
- **A schedule reads by day and time.** Things that happen at a time (runs, classes, bookings) are grouped under day headings ("Today", "Tomorrow", "Sat 2 Nov"), and every card has one compact layout: the date and time in a calendar tile at the start ("SAT" over "2" over "06:30", the time in `heading`), then the title on its own line and the place, distance and pace on the next, at `body` size and shown in full, and the action at the end. On a phone the tile shares its row with the title only, and the place and the other facts start under the tile at the card's full width, so a place name isn't wrapped into the narrow column beside it. Room comes first: a card that cuts every place name off ("Elm Park track…") or shrinks its text to fit reads as cramped. Every card has the same layout whatever its details, at phone and desktop widths alike; cards that wrap their details differently read as unfinished. In its one column a card is as tall as its content, so one that is off is simply shorter. The distance, the pace and the level stay on the facts line (a level as its colour-coded chip), not a row of labelled figures on every card, which makes a schedule long. A phone's first screen is not a dense list of everything, and a desktop's is not one narrow column beside a large empty area.

## Slips that undo it

People comparing two screens notice small slips before they notice style, and one slip can lose a screen that is better in every other way. Check each first-impression and home screen for these:

- **Every label sits the same distance above its field.** A "Forgot password?" link in the password label's row must not make that row taller (see [sign-in](ux-patterns/sign-in.md)).
- **One focus mark.** A focused field changes its border to `focus` or gets a ring flush against it, never a ring with a gap outside a border that still shows: that reads as a double border. Screenshots often catch the first field focused, so check how it looks.
- **Groups, then gaps.** On a short screen like sign-in, things that belong together sit close (the mark, heading and line; each label and field), the gaps between groups are larger, and fields never drift apart to fill the screen. If a phone screen ends with half of it empty, its top is missing something (the illustration or mark the brief asks for), not its gaps. A short phone screen like sign-in starts its group near the top third, under the drawing or mark, and leaves the rest below; a group floated to the middle with a wide band of nothing above it looks as unfinished as one left at the very top.
- **An empty field looks empty.** Placeholder text is `text-secondary` at about half opacity, clearly lighter than what people type, and plainly an example ("e.g. name@uni.edu"); a dark placeholder or a realistic full address reads as one already filled in.
- **Nothing is focused on load.** A sign-in doesn't autofocus its first field: on a phone it throws up the keyboard, and in a screenshot a highlighted empty field looks like an error.
- **Inline text matches.** A question and its link ("New here? Create an account"), or a value and its unit, share one size and one baseline.
- **Item titles are never smaller than their descriptions.** In a feature row or a list item the title is `heading` (or `body` in a heavier weight) and the description is `body` or `caption` in `text-secondary`, never larger than the title above it.
- **Counts match what's shown.** "To do today (3)" lists three items, or ends with "Show all".
- **Rows line up.** Titles start at the same place in every row: a leading badge whose width changes with its word pushes them out of line, so status badges go at the end of the row (or the leading slot gets one fixed width). On a phone, titles wrap to a second line instead of ending in "…"; shorten the copy if they're long.
- **Each action appears once.** "Sign in" in the header and again beside the main action is one too many; the hero's second action is something else ("See how it works") or nothing.
- **The main action clearly outweighs the second one,** by fill as well as position.
- **A button stands clear of its ground.** A filled button whose fill is nearly as light as the ground behind it and of the same hue (a yellow button on a pale-yellow band, a mint one on a mint wash) blends in: give it a `surface` ground, or a band deep enough for the `on-brand` button of [the brand roles](#the-brand-roles). A deep fill on a pale tint of itself is clear enough.
- **A button's label is never small.** A button 44 px or taller, a row's button included, has its label at `body` size or bigger in a heavier weight, never `label` or `caption` size: a small label in a big button reads as faint, and in a shrunk screenshot it can look like dark text on the fill. On a desktop a row's button is as wide as its longest label needs, not a wide block around a short word.
- **No placeholder navigation.** The menu lists only places that exist; an item marked "Soon" or greyed out reads as unfinished.
- **A desktop section fills its width.** A list or a row of cards spans the content width or sits beside a second column of matching height, and a list read in order is one centred column under a header that spans the content width ([working screens](#working-screens)); never a narrow list stranded at one side of an empty area. A single card is the exception: it never stretches across the page, where its few lines leave a long empty row. On a desktop the next-up card sits in the hero beside the headline, in place of the drawing or in its own column next to it, never over it: a card that crops the drawing looks like a layout bug.
- **Columns side by side end together.** A card beside a taller column stretches to the same height (its action pinned to its foot), or the cards sit in one row of equal cards instead; a short card beside a tall stack leaves a hole that looks unfinished.
- **Every control is styled.** A sort or filter control matches the chips beside it (a styled select or segmented control with the active option filled), never the browser's native dropdown; no stray buttons with no clear job (a lone bookmark beside the search); and a card's own buttons never sit over the subject of its drawing.
- **Playful items have a face.** For a playful or gentle brief, each item that is a living thing (a plant, a pet) gets its own small character drawing with a face, and the home can carry one friendly line from it (a speech bubble: "I'm thirsty!"). A task's title starts with its action ("Water Fernanda", "Feed the ficus"), and each kind of task has its colour (water blue, feed green, repot terracotta).
- **A desktop welcome reads from across the room.** Its section headings are `title`, the text under them `body` (never `label` or `caption`), and the line under the headline is larger than body text: a page of uniformly small text looks flat beside one with a clear type scale.
- **Section headings stand clear of the text.** A section's heading is `title` or `heading`, a clear step above `body`, never body size in bold: headings barely bigger than the text under them flatten the page.
- **A stat's label is readable.** Under a big number the label is `body` or `label` size, never `caption`: "runs a week" in tiny grey under "7" reads as an afterthought from across the room.
- **The hero's headline owns its band.** On a desktop the headline is `display` and fills about half the band's width; a small heading floating on a large coloured band looks lost.
- **Drawings show whole.** A hero or panel drawing fits inside its band with room around it, never cut off by the band's edge or the window's; a clipped sun or hill looks like a layout bug.
- **Small grey text is rare.** A row's second line is `label` or `body` in `text-secondary`, not `caption`; `caption` is for a timestamp or a count. On a card, the facts people decide by (the place, the time, who is in, how many are left) are `body` at the regular weight, on a desktop as on a phone: `label` is for chips, tags and a tile's small lines, and a card set all in `label` reads as cramped beside one set in `body`. A screen where most of the text is small and grey looks unfinished.
- **Repeated actions keep their place and shape; their weight says what they do.** Every row's action sits in the same place (the end of the row) at the same width, whatever it says: "Join", "Going ✓" or "Full" share one shape, never one faded until it looks disabled, one that moves below the text in some rows, or one cut off at the screen's edge: the row's text wraps or shortens, the button keeps its width, and nothing ("3 spots left") ever sits under or behind the button. A row's quieter link ("Can't make it") lines up with the row's text. Fill is the weight. Each kind of action has its own step on one ladder, and two different actions don't share a step. **Filled** in `action-primary` is the step forward the list is for (sign up, book, pay, finish a task). **Tonal**, `action-secondary` with `on-action-secondary`, is a lesser or fallback step forward (taking a place in a queue, claiming a job anyone could take). **Outlined**, a `border` line on `surface` with a `text-primary` label, is for looking and opening, and for a step back (step out, give a place up, reopen a finished task, turn an offer down), so stepping back is never as loud as stepping in. When one row is waiting on the person (it is late, or theirs to answer), its action alone is filled and every other action steps down one, so the eye lands there first. The same action looks the same on every row, and a list with three kinds of action shows three steps: every row at one weight, all filled or all outlined, leaves the eye nowhere to start. The steps differ by fill within the action colours, not by a new hue or shadow for each button. A per-row extra (keep, bookmark, share) stays an icon button, off the ladder. Buttons people should press look pressable: tonal is a second step beside a filled button in the same list, its label as heavy as the filled one's, and a list whose only action is pale reads as disabled.
- **Only what can be pressed looks pressable.** A white or outlined pill reads as a button. Facts and features that do nothing are rows with an icon tile, or plain tinted text, never a row of white pills.
- **A control beside a heading sits on its line.** A filter or sort next to a section heading shares the heading's centre line, with its label inside the control ("Pace: All") or as its accessible name, never a label floating above it.
- **Phone rows leave the title room.** If a row's time column and its action leave the title less than half the width, titles wrap word by word: move the time into the row's first line ("Today · 18:30") or the action below the text, so a title wraps at most once.
- **The header lines up with the page.** On a desktop, the header's mark and links sit in the same centred column as the content under them (the same max width and side padding), never pinned to the window's edges above a narrower column.
- **The app is named on every screen.** Its mark or name is in sight on each screen, sign-in included: on a phone, a back arrow with the app's name beside it, or the mark above the heading. A top bar holds at most the mark or name, the language switch and one action; other links (Sign up, Meetups) go in the page or a menu, as a crowded bar reads busy.
- **Every action looks like one.** On a sign-in the main button is the one filled button. Each other sign-in method the app really has is an outlined button of the same width under it; the way in without an account is one quiet text link ("Just looking? See this week's runs"), in `action-primary` with no fill or border, and no row of three or four coloured links competes with the main button. "Forgot password" or "Email me a link" sits small under its field.
- **The sign-in page doesn't offer to sign in again.** Its header carries the mark and a way back, never a "Sign in" button beside the form that already is one.
- **The mark appears once per screen.** A desktop sign-in with a `brand` panel puts the logo on the panel or above the form, not both. The app's name isn't repeated either: with the mark and its name in the top bar, no small line over the headline says the name again.
- **A band ends cleanly.** A coloured band or panel runs to the screen's edges with square corners, or sits inside the page with a margin all round; rounded corners that touch the screen's edge, and a wash that stops in a line beside a button or halfway down a group, show as a seam.
- **One heading per thing.** A page title and a section title right under it that say the same ("This week" over "This week's runs") are one too many: drop one.
- **Avatars are never blank.** Every person shows initials on a tinted circle; a dashed or empty placeholder circle reads as missing data.
- **Numbers use the text face's figures.** Times and counts use the body font with `tabular-nums`, never a monospace or a slashed zero ("1Ø:3Ø" looks like code).
- **Overlapping avatars keep every initial whole.** They overlap by at most a quarter of their width, each with a ring in the surface colour, and the initials stay centred and uncut.
- **Short labels don't break.** Chips, badges and short facts ("Living room", "12 going") never wrap mid-phrase: shorten them, or let the whole chip move to the next line.
- **No stray scaffolding.** No "Demo: name / password" hints, "Lorem", placeholder routes or debug labels on screen; mock sign-in details go in the README.
- **Backgrounds run to the end.** A sidebar's colour reaches the bottom of a long page (let the layout's grid stretch it, with the navigation inside it sticky), and a gradient covers the whole page instead of stopping in a band.

## Before you finish

1. The five-second test passes on the welcome, sign-in and home screens, on a phone and a desktop.
2. Each first-impression screen has its brand moment, as loud as the brief asks.
3. None of the slips above, on any of those screens: look at each screenshot once just for them.
4. Working screens still pass the [visual craft](visual-craft.md) checklist: the brand didn't spill into buttons, links or long lists.
5. Illustrations use role colours only, look right in dark mode, and are hidden from screen readers.
6. **Squint test.** Blur the screenshot (or squint at it): the headline, the main button and the one brand moment still stand out, and nothing else competes with them.
7. **Clutter limit.** At most one depth piece and one drawing per screen, and the accents within the area cap in [visual craft](visual-craft.md).
