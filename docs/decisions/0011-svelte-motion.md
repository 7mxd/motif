# 0011 — Svelte's motion values

- Date: 2026-10-24
- Status: Accepted
- Context: `motif tokens` writes `src/lib/design/motion.ts` for a Svelte 5 app (plan 2J, Task 3). Durations and easing curves carry over directly: Svelte's transitions and `Tween` take milliseconds and an easing function `(t) => number`. Springs don't. A Motif spring role is a duration and a bounce (`spring.snappy`: 0.25 s, bounce 0), the model SwiftUI uses, and `springPhysics` turns it into a mass, a stiffness and a damping for the stacks that need physics (Flutter, Compose, React Native). Svelte's `Spring` (`svelte/motion`, Svelte 5.8 and later) takes a `stiffness` and a `damping` between 0 and 1 that have no unit: they are factors in a step taken once per frame. The question for this spike was which pair makes Svelte's spring move the way the role does, how close it gets, and where it can't. It was worked out from Svelte 5.57.2's own code (`src/motion/spring.js` and `src/internal/client/loop.js`, read from the npm package) and Motion 14.6.0's (`motion-dom`, `animation/generators/spring.mjs`), by simulation in Bun 1.4.3 on Windows 11; the numbers below come from the same simulation the test runs.

## Decisions

1. **How Svelte steps a spring.** Each animation frame, `Spring` computes `dt` = the time since the last frame × 60 / 1000 (so 1 at 60 frames a second; a frame longer than 1/30 s counts as 1/30 s), the velocity (current − last) / `dt`, the acceleration `stiffness` × (target − current) − `damping` × velocity, and moves by (velocity + acceleration) × `dt`. It stops, and jumps to the target, when both that move and the distance left are under `precision` (0.01 by default, in the value's own units). The spike's copy of this step was checked against the real class: Svelte 5.57.2's `Spring`, driven by a fake clock through its `raf` hooks, gave the same values as the copy at every frame (largest difference 9 × 10⁻¹³ over six runs at 60 and 120 frames a second) and stopped on the same frame.
2. **Motif maps a role onto Svelte's step exactly, for 60 frames a second.** At 60 frames a second Svelte's step is a linear recurrence on the distance left, eₙ₊₁ = (2 − damping − stiffness) eₙ − (1 − damping) eₙ₋₁, whose two roots z₁ and z₂ set how it decays and swings. The role's spring (`springPhysics`: ω = 2π / duration, damping ratio ζ = 1 − bounce, or 1 / (1 + bounce) for a negative bounce), looked at once a frame (h = 1/60 s), decays and swings by z = e^(s·h) for each root s of its own equation. Making the two pairs of roots the same gives

   - **stiffness = (1 − z₁)(1 − z₂)** and **damping = 1 − z₁z₂**, where for ζ < 1, z₁z₂ = e^(−2ζωh) and z₁ + z₂ = 2e^(−ζωh)·cos(ω√(1 − ζ²)·h), and for ζ ≥ 1, z = e^(−ω(ζ ∓ √(ζ² − 1))h).

   `svelteSpring` in `cli/src/stacks/svelte/generate.ts` computes this, rounds to four significant digits (the numbers below are for the rounded pairs, as motion.ts has them), and caps stiffness at 1, which is Svelte's own limit. The fixture's springs become `snappy: { stiffness: 0.1171, damping: 0.5673 }`, `smooth: { stiffness: 0.05434, damping: 0.3758 }` and `bouncy: { stiffness: 0.03788, damping: 0.2541 }`.
3. **The result: the role's spring, about half a frame early.** For every spring the presets give (24 roles, 0.2–0.6 s, bounce 0–0.45), Svelte's 2% settling time (below) is within 0.015 of a duration of the role's own and its overshoot within 0.001. Frame by frame, the curve leads the role's by 5.8–7.7 ms (Svelte's first step is a whole frame's: it has moved further than the role after one frame), and with that lead taken out the two are never more than 1.5% of the distance apart. Without it they are up to 8.1% apart, at the start, which is the lead and not the shape. Selected roles (h = one frame, d = the role's duration):

   Below 0.2 s a frame is a sixth of the spring or more, so the steps are coarse and the match loosens (3–6% without the lead). Stiffness reaches Svelte's cap only for springs under 0.1 s with a bounce of 0.3 or more (0.05 s from bounce 0.3, 0.075 s from 0.7); the guide's validator allows 0.05 s, the presets start at 0.2 s.
4. **A fitted pair was tried and not taken.** A search for the pair whose frames come closest to the role's curve at every moment (the smallest largest gap, starting from the pair above) gets closer at the start (snappy: 2.1% apart instead of 6.6%) by making the spring slower to undo the lead. But it no longer settles or overshoots like the role: across 0.2–2 s and bounce −0.5–0.5 its settling time was off by up to 0.57 of a duration and its overshoot by up to 0.027, against 0.047 and 0.003 for the closed form. A spring that ends late or bounces differently is easier to see than one that starts 7 ms early, and the closed form needs no search when the file is written.
5. **What the test holds, and why those limits.** `cli/test/svelte-generate.test.ts` steps each generated pair with the copy of Svelte's step (from 0 to 100, Svelte's default precision) for the 24 preset roles and four more (2 s; 1 s, bounce 0.2; 0.4 s, bounce 0.7; 0.4 s, bounce −0.3), and compares it with the role's spring in closed form:
   - **Settling.** "Settled" is the 2% settling time: the moment after which the value stays within 2% of the distance. It is the usual criterion for a step response, and it fits Motif's durations: a role with bounce 0 is 1.4% from its target at its duration, so it settles at 0.93 of it. The role's spring itself settles anywhere from 0.60 to 0.96 of its duration over the presets (a little bounce settles early, inside the band), so the test compares Svelte's settling time with the role spring's, not with the bare duration, and allows 20% of the duration between them, as the brief asks. Measured: 0.015 at most.
   - **Overshoot.** "About its bounce" is the role spring's overshoot (0.046 for a bounce of 0.3: ζ = 0.7, e^(−πζ/√(1 − ζ²))), within 0.02 of the distance: the same 2%, a difference smaller than the band in which the spring counts as arrived. Measured: 0.001 at most. (The brief's suggestion was 0.05; the data leaves room for the tighter limit.)
   - **The curve.** For some lead between 0 and one frame, every frame is within 2% of the role's spring. Measured: 1.5% at most.
   A pair computed for 50 frames a second instead of 60 fails the test.
6. **The frame rate changes Svelte's springs, and Motif doesn't correct for it.** Svelte's damping works per frame and its stiffness per 60th of a second, so on a faster screen the same pair is a stiffer and more damped spring: at 120 frames a second it behaves like a spring with √2 times the frequency and √2 times the damping ratio. The roles' bounce mostly goes (preset roles at 120 Hz: bouncy 0.046 → 0, playful's bouncy 0.126 → 0.020) and settling moves from 0.58–0.97 to 0.56–1.13 of the duration (snappy 0.93 → 1.13; bouncy 0.97 → 0.65). At 144 Hz it is much the same; at 30 Hz, or whenever frames take 1/30 s or longer, the bounce grows (bouncy: 0.169, settling 1.80 durations). There is no pair that is right at every rate, and Svelte can't be told the rate, so motion.ts targets 60, the rate Svelte's own step is written for, and says so in its comment, pointing to `motionSpring` for a spring that is the same on every screen.
7. **`motionSpring` is React web's spring, for Motion's `animate()`.** motion.ts also exports each role as Motion takes it, `{ type: "spring", visualDuration, bounce }`, in seconds, exactly as React web's and Vue's motion.ts give it. Motion steps its springs by the clock, not by frames, so they don't change with the screen. They aren't the same curve as the role's, though: Motion 14.6.0 reads `visualDuration` as ω = 2π / (1.2 × visualDuration) with ζ = 1 − bounce, so its springs take 1.2 times as long as Motif's model (2% settling over the presets: 0.72–1.15 of the duration against the role's 0.60–0.96). Motif passes the role's duration through unchanged, as React web always has; so in a Svelte app `spring.snappy` (Svelte's `Spring`, following the role as Flutter, Compose and React Native do) settles about a sixth sooner than `motionSpring.snappy` (as on React web and Vue).
8. **Durations and easings.** `duration` is in milliseconds, the unit of `transition:fly={{ duration }}` and `Tween`. `easing` holds functions made from each role's cubic-bezier points by a solver in the file (no package): Newton's method from x = t, eight steps at most to within 10⁻⁷, then halving when it stalls or steps off the curve, as browsers solve CSS's `cubic-bezier()`; 0 and 1 at the ends exactly. The test checks every role at 11 points against a separate 60-halving solver (within 10⁻⁶), CSS's `ease` at 0.5 (0.8024033877) and a curve that goes past 1.

## Consequences

- A Svelte app gets springs that move like the guide's on iOS, Android and Flutter (Decision 3) at 60 frames a second, and a little stiffer and calmer on a 120 Hz screen (Decision 6). An app that needs the same motion on every screen uses `motionSpring` with Motion's `animate()`.
- The two springs in motion.ts are deliberately not the same curve (Decision 7). If Motif later corrects Motion's 1.2 (passing `visualDuration: duration / 1.2`, say), that changes React web and Vue too and belongs to its own decision.
- `Spring`'s `precision` is left at Svelte's default, 0.01 in the value's own units: a spring from 0 to 1 (an opacity, a scale) jumps the last 1%, inside the 2% band; one in pixels runs on below what a screen shows.
- Svelte 4 and SvelteKit 1 have no `Spring` or `Tween` classes, so they get the CSS files and a note instead of motion.ts.
- The test's copy of Svelte's step is Svelte 5.57.2's. If Svelte changes how `Spring` steps (making it frame-rate independent, for one), the mapping and the test change with it.
