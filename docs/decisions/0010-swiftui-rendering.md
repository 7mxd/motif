# 0010 — Rendering SwiftUI screens

- Date: 2026-10-21
- Status: Accepted
- Context: `motif shot` must show a SwiftUI app's screens the way people see them, in every size, light and dark, right to left and in each design variant, without editing the developer's project and without a Simulator window. SwiftUI only draws on Apple's platforms, so this needs a Mac with Xcode. This spike (plan 2H, Task 6) ran on the build Mac mini (Apple M6, macOS 27.0, Xcode 27.0, Swift 6.4, the iOS 27.0 simulator runtime, the "iPhone 18 Pro" device type) on `cli/test/fixtures/swiftui-min`, grown for it into the Xcode 16 "App" template with two screens in a `NavigationStack`, three `#Preview`s (one named), English and Arabic strings in `Localizable.xcstrings`, and a bundled Noto Naskh Arabic registered by `UIAppFonts`. It can be drawn, by a unit-test target hosted by the app in a simulator Motif creates. The harness is `cli/src/stacks/swiftui/harness/` (three Swift files and the scheme at the spike, five Swift files now, embedded as text), and its runner is `cli/src/stacks/swiftui/harness.ts`. `cli/test/swiftui-render.test.ts` draws two apps. swiftui-min, from a path with a space and an `&`, with Motif's generated theme dropped in: the root in light and dark, a preview right to left in Arabic, one at the tablet size, and a theme probe in the default and partner variants (and in a variant the theme lacks, which must fail). And `cli/test/fixtures/swiftui-xcode26`, Xcode 26's template with SwiftData (main actor by default, a titled `WindowGroup` with `.modelContainer`, an `@Previewable` preview, and a preview that stops the app). Google fonts, Dynamic Type, the other sizes and the safe area were drawn by hand during the spike and looked at; Tasks 9 and 10 test them end to end. Fix round 1 (after review and CI's first run) changed 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9 and 10 and added 12–15.

## Decisions

1. **Screens are drawn in the iOS simulator by an XCTest unit-test target hosted by the app (`MotifShots`), in a copy of the project.** The test runs in the app's own process, so `Bundle.main`, its string catalogues, asset catalogues and `UIAppFonts` fonts, its `UIApplication` and its window scene are the app's. For each shot the harness (`MotifShotTests.testShots`, reading the options file) makes the view, wraps it, hosts it and draws it:
   - **Hosting.** A `UIHostingController` is the root of a new `UIWindow` on the app's window scene, framed at the shot's size in points. The window's `traitOverrides` set `userInterfaceStyle`, `layoutDirection`, `horizontalSizeClass` (regular from 600 points wide, compact below), `verticalSizeClass` (regular), `displayScale` and, when asked, `preferredContentSizeCategory`. The view gets `.environment(\.locale, …)` and `.environment(\.layoutDirection, …)`.
   - **Settling.** The main run loop turns in 50 ms steps until the window's layer tree (frames and sublayer counts) is the same twice in a row, at most `settle` seconds (5 by default). Every screen in the spike settled in the minimum: a shot took 0.21–0.35 s. **The loop notices layout only.** A change that moves no layer and adds none (a colour or an opacity animating, text replaced in a label of the same size, an image loaded into a frame already laid out, a `.task` that sets state without changing sizes) passes for settled, and the shot can show the screen before it. Task 8 may wait for the layers' contents too, or for the main queue to be idle; this was not measured.
   - **Drawing.** `window.drawHierarchy(in:afterScreenUpdates: true)` into a `UIGraphicsImageRenderer` at the shot's scale, opaque, `preferredRange = .standard` (8 bits a channel, sRGB, whatever the simulated screen's range), written as PNG.
   - **Not `ImageRenderer`.** Rendered alongside in the spike, it drew the whole `NavigationStack` screen, and the `Toggle` in another, as SwiftUI's yellow placeholder with a red "no" sign: it can't draw views backed by UIKit (`NavigationStack`, `Form`'s rows, `Toggle`, `TextField`). `drawHierarchy` drew all of them, `Form` and its insets, the navigation bar's large title and the switch included.
   - **Strings and direction from the environment.** `\.locale` set to `ar` alone made `Text("Welcome")` look up the Arabic in `Localizable.xcstrings` ("أهلا بك", the title "الرئيسية", the button "افتح الإعدادات"): no relaunch, no `-AppleLanguages`, no `-testLanguage`. With `\.layoutDirection` and the trait set to right to left, `VStack(alignment: .leading)`, `Form` rows, the switch and the large title all mirrored.
   - **Sizes.** The four native sizes are Flutter's and Compose's, phones at 3× (a phone's own scale) and the tablet at 2×: `mobile-360` (360×800) gives 1080×2400, `mobile` (390×844) 1170×2532, `mobile-430` (430×932) 1290×2796, `tablet` (768×1024) 1536×2048. All four were drawn on the one phone simulator and looked at; a window larger than the phone's screen (the tablet) is drawn whole. The tablet gets the regular size class (the navigation bar's iPad layout appeared), but the idiom stays the phone's: anything an app decides by `userInterfaceIdiom` is drawn as on a phone.
   - **The safe area is the shot's, not the simulator's.** A window on the iPhone 18 Pro got a top inset of 62 and a bottom of 4 (the window is shorter than the screen), which would change with the simulator Motif picks. So the host ignores the container's safe area (`safeAreaRegions = []`) and the view gets the shot's as `.safeAreaPadding`. Phones get 47 at the top and 34 at the bottom, as Flutter's and Compose's shots do; the tablet and previews get none. Proven with a probe preview (a blue background ignoring the safe area behind red content): the blue was 141 px at the top and 102 at the bottom at 3×, 47 and 34 points. No status bar is drawn.
   - **Dynamic Type** is the `preferredContentSizeCategory` trait: `UICTContentSizeCategoryAccessibilityL` enlarged the system text and a `Font.custom` text alike, and the body wrapped onto two lines.
   - **Fallback: the window's layers.** When the app has no window scene (a Swift package's tests, 3), or `drawHierarchy` answers false, the window's `layer.render(in:)` is drawn instead, and the result's notes say blurs and materials may be missing. In a package's tests `drawHierarchy` printed "[Snapshotting] Render server returned error for view (…, UIWindow)" and gave a blank picture, while `layer.render` drew the `NavigationStack`, its large title, the `Form` and the switch.
2. **The harness goes in by writing the project file's objects directly, from TypeScript (`addTestTarget` in `harness.ts`): no Ruby gem, no separate project.**
   - **Why not the `xcodeproj` gem.** This Mac has `/usr/bin/ruby` and Homebrew's Ruby 4.0.7, and no `xcodeproj` gem. Every developer would need it installed per user (`gem install --user-install xcodeproj`), with its own Ruby, before their first screenshot, and the gem follows Xcode's new object versions (77, with synchronized folders) some time after Xcode ships them. What Motif needs from it is small and additive.
   - **Why not a separate harness project.** It would have to rebuild the app's target by hand: its build settings and configurations, its packages, resources, asset catalogues, string catalogues, Info.plist, entitlements and build phases. A copy of the app's own project builds the app exactly as Xcode does, and the test target only adds to it.
   - **Where.** The harness's sources, the options, `result.json` and (unless the caller names another) xcodebuild's DerivedData go in the run's work folder (`workDir`), a folder Motif owns outside the copy. The copy gets only the patched project file and the scheme. Not inside the copy: a synchronized folder takes every file under it into the app target, hidden folders included. Proven: a `.hidden/Bad.swift` inside the fixture's synchronized `SwiftUIMin` folder failed the app's build ("Expressions are not allowed at the top level (in target 'SwiftUIMin' …)"), so a project whose synchronized folder is its root would have built the harness, and DerivedData's generated Swift, into the app.
   - **What is written,** into the copy only. The project file gets, appended to its `objects`: a `PBXGroup` named `MotifShots` for the work folder's sources (its absolute path, `sourceTree = "<absolute>"`) with a plain `PBXFileReference` and `PBXBuildFile` per Swift file, so it works whatever the object version; sources, frameworks and resources build phases; a `PBXContainerItemProxy` and `PBXTargetDependency` on the app target; the `.xctest` product reference; one `XCBuildConfiguration` per configuration the project has; and the `PBXNativeTarget` `MotifShots` (`com.apple.product-type.bundle.unit-test`). The target is added to the project's `targets` and the group to its main group. Ids are `4D4F54494600` ("MOTIF") and a counter, 24 hex digits, skipping any the file already has. The scheme `MotifShots` goes in `<project>.xcodeproj/xcshareddata/xcschemes/` (it builds the app for testing and tests `MotifShots`, in the configuration of 14). A project that already has a `MotifShots` target is refused: the harness goes into a fresh copy each run. `motif check`'s detection still finds the app target in the patched file (`swiftui-harness.test.ts`).
   - **The target's settings:** `TEST_HOST = $(BUILT_PRODUCTS_DIR)/<FULL_PRODUCT_NAME>/$(BUNDLE_EXECUTABLE_FOLDER_PATH)/<EXECUTABLE_NAME>`, `BUNDLE_LOADER = $(TEST_HOST)`, `GENERATE_INFOPLIST_FILE = YES`, `PRODUCT_BUNDLE_IDENTIFIER = dev.motif.shots`, `SWIFT_VERSION = 5.0`, `SWIFT_DEFAULT_ACTOR_ISOLATION = nonisolated`, `SWIFT_STRICT_CONCURRENCY = minimal`, `CODE_SIGNING_ALLOWED = NO`, `ENABLE_USER_SCRIPT_SANDBOXING = NO`, and the app target's own `IPHONEOS_DEPLOYMENT_TARGET`, `MACOSX_DEPLOYMENT_TARGET`, `SDKROOT`, `SUPPORTED_PLATFORMS` and `TARGETED_DEVICE_FAMILY`. These, and `PRODUCT_MODULE_NAME` for `@testable import`, `FULL_PRODUCT_NAME` and `EXECUTABLE_NAME`, come from `xcodebuild -showBuildSettings -json -project … -scheme MotifShots -configuration <14> -destination "generic/platform=iOS Simulator" -derivedDataPath …` on the copy (0.5–1.4 s), the app target's entry of its standard output. They are read through the harness's own scheme because xcodebuild takes `-derivedDataPath` only with a scheme: with `-target`, it left a small `SwiftUIMin-<hash>` folder in `~/Library/Developer/Xcode/DerivedData` for every copy. So the harness is written first with the target's name for the module and product, then written again, from the untouched project file, with xcodebuild's values. Only standard output is read: xcodebuild's warnings ("Using the first of multiple matching destinations") go to standard error. `swiftui-render.test.ts` checks that no such folder is left.
   - **The run** is `xcodebuild test -project <copy>/<App>.xcodeproj -scheme MotifShots -configuration <14> -destination id=<udid> -derivedDataPath … -collect-test-diagnostics never CODE_SIGNING_ALLOWED=NO COMPILER_INDEX_STORE_ENABLE=NO ENABLE_TESTABILITY=YES`, with `TEST_RUNNER_MOTIF_SHOT_OPTIONS=<workDir>/options.json` in its environment. `-collect-test-diagnostics never` is needed: after a crash (12), and after a SwiftData runtime warning in a test that passed, Xcode 27's xcodebuild ran `simctl diagnose` and waited for it: "Failure collecting diagnostics from simulator: Timed out after 600.0 seconds". Xcode 27's `xcodebuild -help` lists the flag, and CI's Xcode 16.4 accepts it. xcodebuild hands variables starting `TEST_RUNNER_` to the test process without the prefix. A simulator's processes read and write the Mac's own files, so the PNGs and `result.json` (written whole: `result.json.part`, then moved) go straight to their folders.
   - **Proven on two projects,** both drawn by `swiftui-render.test.ts`. The fixture swiftui-min (Xcode 16's template: object version 77, its folder a synchronized group with an exception set for `Info.plist`, Swift 5, iOS 17). And `swiftui-xcode26`, Xcode 26's template with SwiftData, hand-written since xcodebuild can't create projects: `SWIFT_DEFAULT_ACTOR_ISOLATION = MainActor` (on the target; in the spike also on the project, which the harness target overrides), `SWIFT_APPROACHABLE_CONCURRENCY`, `SWIFT_UPCOMING_FEATURE_MEMBER_IMPORT_VISIBILITY`, `STRING_CATALOG_GENERATE_SYMBOLS`, an `@Observable` model held in the App's `@State` and passed with `.environment(model)`, a SwiftData `@Query`, and an `@Previewable @State` preview. Its deployment target is iOS 18, not the template's 26, so CI's floor builds it; Xcode 16.4 ignores the settings it doesn't know, so its main actor by default is proven on Xcode 26 and later only. Member import visibility is strict: the fixture's `ObservableObject` needed `import Combine` (without it: "Initializer 'init(wrappedValue:)' is not available due to missing import of defining module 'Combine'"). Both built and drew without warnings from the harness's files.
   - **Not tried:** projects older than object version 70 (Xcode 15 and before, groups instead of synchronized folders; the patch uses only objects they have too), workspaces and CocoaPods, Swift package dependencies (resolved again in the copy), several app targets, and app extensions.
3. **A Swift package app: proven in pieces, not yet drawn by `harness.ts`** (which refuses one, for Task 8). On a package with an iOS 17 executable target holding the `@main` App:
   - the copy's `Package.swift` gets one line appended, `package.targets.append(.testTarget(name: "MotifShots", dependencies: [.target(name: "<app>")], path: "MotifShots"))`, which SwiftPM accepts after the `Package(…)` call; the developer's manifest is never parsed or rewritten;
   - the folder must not be hidden: with `path: ".motif-shots"` SwiftPM silently left the target out (`swift package describe` didn't list it, and xcodebuild said "Scheme PkgApp is not currently configured for the test action");
   - `xcodebuild test -scheme <package's scheme> -destination id=…` ran it, and `@testable import` of the executable target worked;
   - the tests run in a bare `xctest` process with no app and no window scene (`connectedScenes.count` was 0), so the screen is drawn from its layers (1). Its own fonts aren't registered by any Info.plist; Task 8 refuses Swift package apps instead (§16), so this was never needed. The harness already looks for previews in the test bundle's own image, where a package's code is linked.
4. **Screens: `#Preview`s are found at run time; the root is the App's `WindowGroup`, found with `Mirror`.**
   - **Previews.** `#Preview` expands to a struct conforming to `DeveloperToolsSupport.PreviewRegistry` (`static var fileID`, `line`, `column`, `@MainActor static func makePreview() throws -> Preview`), whose name no source can spell (`$s10SwiftUIMin…PreviewRegistryfMu_`). Swift records every conformance in the `__TEXT,__swift5_proto` section of the image that declares it. `MotifPreviews.all()` reads that section (`getsectiondata`) in every loaded image inside the app's bundle (the executable and, in Debug, Xcode 16's `<App>.debug.dylib`, where the code is) or the test bundle, keeps the conformances whose protocol is named `PreviewRegistry`, calls each non-generic type's metadata accessor, and casts it to `any PreviewRegistry.Type`. A preview's id is `<fileID>:<line>` (`SwiftUIMin/SettingsScreen.swift:20`, the line of `#Preview`); its name is the `Preview`'s `displayName` ("Settings"). `fileID`'s first part is the module (`PRODUCT_MODULE_NAME`), which Motif's discovery in the source (Task 7) doesn't know before xcodebuild says, and its second part is the file's name alone, without its folders (`HomeScreen.swift`, though the file is in `Screens/`). So a shot names a preview by `<file name>:<line>` (`HomeScreen.swift:26`), which Task 7 computes from the source, and the harness matches it against the ids' ends (`MotifPreviews.matching`; the full id works too). A Swift module can't have two files of one name, so file and line are unique. All three of the fixture's were found, and the template copy's fourth.
   - **A preview's view.** `Preview` is opaque. `Mirror` shows its body as a closure, `dataSource.preview.structure.singlePreview.makeBody: @MainActor () -> ViewPreviewBody`, with `ViewPreviewBody` private to SwiftUI. `MotifMirror.call` calls a closure that takes nothing whatever it returns: the result type comes from the closure's function-type metadata (kind `0x302`; the flags, then the result type, a word each), the closure is cast to `@MainActor () -> T` with `T` opened from it (`_openExistential`), and called. The view is then the first value inside the result that is a `View` (an `Optional` is looked through). `@Previewable` worked: its view is the macro's `__P_Previewable_Transform_Wrapper`, which holds the `@State`.
   - **Not `_PreviewHost`.** SwiftUI's `_PreviewHost.makeHost(providerType:)` answered nothing for these types: it reads the older `PreviewProvider`. Whether it draws a `PreviewProvider` wasn't tried (Task 7).
   - **This rests on layouts that aren't API** (the conformance records, which are ABI; `Preview`'s stored properties, which aren't). If a later Xcode moves them, the preview fails with "Motif couldn't find the view in the preview at <id>. Tell Motif's maintainers which Xcode you use." The way left is Motif's own: compile each `#Preview` body into the copy, appended to its own file so it reaches `fileprivate` types, as a function the harness calls.
   - **Preview traits** (`#Preview(traits: .landscapeLeft)`, `.sizeThatFitsLayout`) are ignored: a preview is drawn at the shot's size. A `#Preview` of a UIKit view controller has no `View` inside and fails with the message above.
   - **The root (`/`)** is `<App>().body`: `MotifMirror.windowContent` walks the scene for the first `WindowGroup<…>` and reads only its `content` property, which SwiftUI keeps as a closure (`content: WindowGroupRootContent<…>.lazy: () -> ContentView`), and calls it; content kept as the view itself would be taken too. Until fix round 1 it took the first `View` anywhere inside the `WindowGroup`: for `WindowGroup("My App") { ContentView() }` that was the title, and the shot showed "My App" alone in the middle of a white screen (seen). When the content can't be found, the shot fails with "Motif couldn't find the view inside <App>'s WindowGroup. Draw its screens from previews (--screen) instead.", never another picture. Each shot's `view` in `result.json` is the drawn view's type (`ModifiedContent<ContentView, _EnvironmentKeyWritingModifier<Optional<AppModel>>>`), which the render test checks. Modifiers inside the `WindowGroup` come with it (the template copy's root was `ContentView().environment(model).motifTheme()`).
   - **Scene modifiers outside the `WindowGroup` aren't applied, and that can stop the app** (as the spike found; superseded by §16: they are applied now, all but SwiftData's `.modelContainer(for:)`, which the run's note names). `.modelContainer(…)` on the `WindowGroup`, an `.environment(…)` or `.environmentObject(…)` there, are lost. What happens then is the view's: SwiftData's template (`@Query` with the container on the `WindowGroup`) drew an empty list ("Items 0") on iOS 27, with SwiftData's runtime warning "Set a .modelContext in view's environment to use Query", but a view that inserts into the context, or reads an `@EnvironmentObject` given only there, stops the app with a fatal error, which 12 turns into that screen's failure. Task 8 should say so in the screen's error, and could find a `ModelContainer` in the scene with `Mirror` and give it to the root. Making the App again runs its `init`, and its `@State` is read outside a view: SwiftUI warned "Accessing State<AppModel>'s value without being installed on a View. This will create a new AppModel instance each time." The app's real launch also runs first, as the test's host. **Fixed in plan 2H Task 15's follow-up and its final review:** on Apple's Landmarks that new instance each time lost what the root's own `.onGeometryChange` wrote into the App's `@State` model (the window's size), so the home was drawn without its featured header. The App is declared a `DynamicProperty` in the run's own file (`extension <App>: DynamicProperty {}`), so a view that holds it (`MotifAppKeeper`, rendered by an `ImageRenderer` kept until the next root view) has SwiftUI install the App's `@State` as its own: `State<…>` on Xcode 16's SDK, `LazyState<…>` (its initial value a thunk) on Xcode 27's, each with a `_location` once installed. **A new App is made for each root view** (`MotifAppState.app()`), so its `init` runs and its `@State` starts fresh for each screenshot of `/`, on every SDK. The follow-up first made the App once per launch: on Xcode 27, whose `LazyState` runs its thunk for each install, that still gave each root a fresh model, but with Xcode 16's eager `State` (and with `State(initialValue:)` on any SDK) the one value made with the App was shared by every root shot, so what one shot did to it (a sign-in, a counter) carried into the next. Before that, one model for the whole launch had carried what the iPad's first layout wrote into Landmarks's model (its inspector shown) into every later screenshot. A `@State` that still isn't installed (its `_location` empty) is named in the run's note. The App's other property wrappers are installed with it: a delegate adaptor (`@UIApplicationDelegateAdaptor`, `@NSApplicationDelegateAdaptor`), a `@StateObject` and `@Environment(\.scenePhase)` draw `/` without trouble, proven by the e2e tests on both platforms (in CI on Xcode 16.4 too); the adaptor's delegate isn't the app's (its launch work ran once, in the real launch). Proven by `swiftui-shot-e2e.test.ts`: the App's store, made with `State(initialValue:)` and counted up by the root's `.onAppear`, reads "Store opened 1" in every root screenshot (it read 2, 4… with one App per launch), and on Landmarks (the header drawn at every size, scheme, variant and direction).
   - **Routes** (a `NavigationStack` path) weren't tried: the root is drawn as it starts. Tasks 7 and 8.
5. **Variants, tint and the main actor (Task 3's review notes).**
   - **`MotifTheme.debugVariantOverride` is set before each shot's view is made and hosted** (`MotifShotApp.setVariant`, with the guide's id, through `MotifVariant(rawValue:)`), for the default's shots too. It is a plain static, which SwiftUI doesn't observe: set after the view was hosted and settled, the shot still showed the default (the spike's "late" shot), so it only works before the first render, and the harness sets it there. Proven by eye: the probe's surface was white for `default` and `#FFF7ED` for `partner`, light and dark, and the default's shot set to `"default"` explicitly was the default. `swiftui-render.test.ts` checks that the partner probe differs from the default's. **A variant the app's `MotifTheme.swift` doesn't have** (the guide gained one since `motif tokens`) fails that shot with "The app's MotifTheme.swift has no variant nope. Run motif tokens again, then motif shot.", rather than drawing the app's own variant; the screen's other shots are taken. An app without variants given one fails the same way, even "default": Task 8 leaves `variant` out of the shots when the guide has no variants (`SwiftUIShot.variant` says so).
   - **Tint.** The generated theme's tint comes from its modifier (`.tint(colors.actionPrimary)`), not from the asset catalogue's `AccentColor`, so a preview that doesn't call `.motifTheme()` would be drawn in the asset's accent (the system blue, in the fixture's first shots). The harness wraps every screen in `.motifTheme()` when the app has the generated theme (`MotifShotApp.themed`, outermost, so an app's own inner `.motifTheme()` still applies, and reads the same override). Proven: the bordered button and the switch of a preview without `.motifTheme()` came out in the guide's indigo, and in its lighter dark-mode indigo in dark mode.
   - **The main actor.** The harness's own target is built in Swift 5 with `SWIFT_DEFAULT_ACTOR_ISOLATION = nonisolated` and minimal checking, whatever the app uses; the test class and every type that touches the app's views are `@MainActor`, so they can call an app whose types are main-actor by default (Xcode 26's template). Proven with the template-shaped copy: it built and drew. Xcode 16.4 doesn't know `SWIFT_DEFAULT_ACTOR_ISOLATION` and ignores it, as it does in `swiftui-compile.test.ts`; CI's job (10) confirms it.
6. **Fonts.**
   - A font the app bundles and lists in `UIAppFonts` is there in the hosted test: the fixture's Noto Naskh Arabic was drawn by `Font.custom("Noto Naskh Arabic", size: 22)`.
   - **Google families** were registered for the process by the spike (superseded by §16: `motif shot` doesn't register them, so the screenshots show the system font until the app bundles the family, as the app does), before the first shot, with `CTFontManagerRegisterFontsForURL(url, .process, …)`, from the files the options list (Motif's cache, `motifHome()/fonts/google/<Family>/`, as for Flutter and Compose). The spike registered Onest, Inter and Readex Pro (variable TTFs from `google/fonts`): `.motifFont(.body)` drew Onest, `.motifFont(.label)` Inter, and right to left the body's Arabic companion, Readex Pro. Unlike Compose, no override hook in the theme is needed: `Font.custom` looks the family up by name when it draws. A file that can't be registered gives a note.
7. **Line height: 1.2 is right for the system font only** (Task 3's review note). Measured in the simulator: the advance between two lines of `Text("Ag\nAg")` (its height less one line's) equals `UIFont.lineHeight` to the pixel, and `.lineSpacing(10)` added exactly 10 points to it.

   | Font | 12 | 16 | 20 | 28 | 40 | Ratio |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | System (SF Pro) | 14.32 | 19.09 | 23.87 | 33.41 | 47.73 | 1.193 |
   | Inter | 14.52 | 19.36 | 24.20 | 33.88 | 48.40 | 1.210 |
   | Readex Pro | 15.00 | 20.00 | 25.00 | 35.00 | 50.00 | 1.250 |
   | Onest | 15.30 | 20.40 | 25.50 | 35.70 | 51.00 | 1.275 |
   | Noto Naskh Arabic | 20.44 | 27.25 | 34.06 | 47.68 | 68.12 | 1.703 |

   The generated `lineSpacing = size × (lineHeight − 1.2)` is within about 0.1 point for the system font, but too loose by 1.2 points for Onest at 16 (a 1.5 body gets 25.2 points a line instead of 24) and by 8 points for Noto Naskh at 16. The space SwiftUI needs is `lineHeight × size − UIFont(name:size:).lineHeight`, which only the font knows. Task 3's code (not changed here) should compute it where the font is: in `MotifType`, at run time from `UIFont(name:size:)` (`NSFont` on macOS), scaled with the text through `UIFontMetrics` so Dynamic Type keeps the ratio; or at generate time from the font file's `hhea` ascender, descender and line gap over its units per em, for project fonts and cached Google families, with 1.2 kept for the system font.

   **Done in Task 11's fix round 1, at run time, with Dynamic Type for every role.** `MotifType` now carries the guide's line height as a multiple, with its size, tracking, weight, text style, family (nil for the system font) and design. `.motifFont` scales the size with a `ScaledMetric` relative to the role's text style, so a role in the system font follows Dynamic Type too (before, `Font.system(size:)` kept its size). It draws `Font.custom(name, fixedSize:)` or `Font.system(size:weight:design:)` at that size, and scales letter spacing with it. Line spacing is `lineHeight × size − the font's own line height`: `UIFont(name:size:)` (or the family by descriptor), and on the Mac `NSFont`'s ascender − descender + leading. 1.2 × the size is used only when the font can't be loaded. Proven on the Mac mini (Xcode 27, iOS 27 simulator, macOS 27). A probe compiled with the generated theme rendered 1 and 11 lines of `Text(…).motifFont(…)` with `ImageRenderer` at 4×, which gives the advance between lines:

   | Role | Font's own line | Target | iOS advance | macOS advance | Before (1.2 rule) |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | Noto Naskh Arabic 16, line height 2.0 | 27.25 | 32 | 32.00 | 31.77 | 40.05 |
   | System 16, line height 1.5 | 19.09 (iOS), 18.84 (Mac) | 24 | 24.00 | 24.18 | 23.89 |

   On iOS the font's line height is the table's above to the hundredth, and the advance is exact. On the Mac SwiftUI's line is about 0.2 points off `NSFont`'s ascender − descender + leading, so the advance is within 0.25 points. Dynamic Type, on iOS: the system-font role's one line went 19.25 → 25.5 → 42 points at Large, xxxLarge and accessibility3, and its advance 24 → 31.88 → 52.5 (1.5 times the scaled size each time); Noto Naskh's advance went 32 → 42.5 → 70. The Mac has no Dynamic Type: its sizes stayed put. `swiftui-compile.test.ts` typechecks the new API, and `swiftui-guide-compile.test.ts` the guide's examples against it.
8. **The simulator: Motif's own, created and deleted by each run, with no window.**

12. **A screen that stops the app fails alone, with its reason; the run goes on** (fix round 1). A `fatalError` in a screen (an `@EnvironmentObject` nobody provides, a precondition, a crash in the app's code) ends the app, and with it the test. Before, `result.json` was written after the last shot, so the whole run came back with no result and no reason.
   - **The blame goes to `current`**, the shot the harness last started. A fault that comes late, from work a screen started that finishes after its shot was taken (a `Task`, a timer, a network callback), stops the app while the next shot is being drawn, and that next screen is blamed. The reason (the `Fatal error` or exception text) usually names the real code; Task 8 should say so in the error when the reason doesn't belong to the blamed screen's file.
   - **A screen that never finishes** (an endless loop in `body`, a semaphore wait on the main thread) holds the app until the run's time runs out (fix round 2): the run returns `timedOut`, keeps the shots before it, and names it: "The screenshots took longer than 600 s and were stopped while drawing Hang.swift:5 (hang.png); the shots before it were kept. Check that screen for work that never ends, or run motif shot again with a longer time limit." The screens after it aren't drawn.
   - Besides Swift's `Fatal error:`, an Objective-C exception's "Terminating app due to uncaught exception '…', reason: '…'" is read as the reason (fix round 2).
   - The harness writes `result.json` (whole, through `result.json.part`) before the first shot, before each shot with that shot as `current`, and after it, and at the end with `done: true`.
   - xcodebuild, after the crash, prints "Restarting after unexpected exit, crash, or test timeout" and runs the tests that are left: none, since all shots are one test. So Motif reads the last `result.json`: its shots are kept, `current` names the screen that stopped the app, and the reason is the `Fatal error: …` line of xcodebuild's output (`crashReason`). Every shot of that screen fails with "The preview BrokenPreview.swift:23 stopped the app: Fatal error: No ObservableObject of type Session found. A View.environmentObject(_:) for Session may be missing as an ancestor of this view." (or "The app's root view stopped the app: …"), which also goes in the result's notes, and the screens left are drawn by `xcodebuild test-without-building` on the same simulator, as many launches as it takes. Proven on the Mac by the Xcode 26 fixture's broken preview, and in CI by the same test.
   - No `result.json` at all is never a quiet `null`: a build that failed gives "The app didn't build with Motif's harness:" and the compiler's errors; a test that ran without its options (skipped) says so; an app that stopped before writing anything says "xcodebuild ran the harness, but it wrote no result:" with xcodebuild's last lines; a result with no `current` says the app stopped before its first screen.

The Mac: `MotifAX` by selector (17), roles `AXStaticText`, `AXButton` and its kin, `AXLink`, `AXCheckBox`/`AXSwitch`, `AXImage`, `AXTextField` and its kin, `AXSlider`; frames from the screen to the window (bottom-left) and flipped. A Form row's label names its unnamed control (17), so a grouped Form isn't flagged.
    - **What the elements say, seen in the probes.** SwiftUI's elements carry their text's font and colour: iOS in the attributed label (`UIAccessibilityTokenFontSize` 34 and `UIAccessibilityTokenFontName` `.SFUI-Bold` for a bold large title, 12 for a caption, `UIAccessibilityTokenForegroundColor`); the Mac in `accessibilityAttributedStringForRange:` (`AXFont` with `AXFontSize` and `AXFontName`, `AXForegroundColor` a `CGColor`). A Button's label says black although its label is drawn in the tint, so the colour is only a hint.
    - **Contrast.** The PNG is decoded (ImageIO, 8-bit sRGB). Under an element's frame, the most common colour is the background. The foreground is the element's own colour when that colour (blended at its alpha) is in the picture as a glyph's core (as many pixels as the floor below) and isn't within twice the tolerance of the background (fix round 1: before, three pixels within 12 of it were enough). Else it is, of the colours drawn often enough to be a glyph's core (a tenth of the most common one's pixels), the one that stands out most. As Flutter's audit does, the background must cover half the pixels that aren't glyphs and their anti-aliased edges, else the text isn't judged and the screen gets a note ("Contrast on X wasn't judged for 1 text with no one colour behind it (a picture or a gradient): check it by eye."). Nor is it when the colour found that way is barely apart from the background (under 1.5:1) while colours that stand out more, not on the line between the two, add up to a glyph's core (plan 2H Task 15's hands-on): on Apple's Landmarks a segmented control's selected segment holds more of the grey track than of its black label's cores, and a glass "Edit" button's sheen more than any one shade of its indigo text, and both were "1.03–1.16:1" before. On CI's floor (iOS 18.5, macOS 15) the planted segmented picker still failed (run 39719894880: "Map" 2.51:1 in the simulator's dark, "Map" 2.21:1 and 2.63:1 and "Activities" 2:1 on the Mac): there the selected segment's fill and the track are 2–3:1 apart, beyond that rule. So a segment of a segmented control (a view inside a `UISegmentedControl`, AppKit's `NSAccessibilitySegment`, whose parent is the control's cell, as SwiftUI's segmented `Picker` gives on the Mac) is judged only by its own colour, as a switch's label is (`strict`); without it, it isn't judged. Judged: texts (as `element`), a text button's or link's label, a field's typed text (not its prompt, as axe-core leaves placeholders), a toggle's label only when its own colour is found (its row holds the switch too); not a disabled control, nor text without a letter or digit. Large text (3:1) is 24 pt, or 18.66 pt and bold (a font named bold, heavy or black, not semibold), from the element's font. Without one: a text's size from its lines' height (a line is about 1.2 times the size); a control's from its ink (fix round 1), the tallest run of rows with its text's colour, about 0.95 times the size, as a bordered button's frame holds its padding. Not bold either way. **Dynamic Type (fix round 1, asked by the review):** the font size an element says is the size drawn: a body `Text` at `.dynamicTypeSize(.accessibility3)` said `UIAccessibilityTokenFontSize` 40, not 17 (the probe set Dynamic Type through the environment; Motif's own content-size trait, which `motif shot` doesn't set yet, wasn't tried). So large text needs no scaling. **Icons:** a control whose element shows an SF Symbol is judged at 3:1 as `kind: "icon"` with the symbol's name, as Flutter's icons are; an image that isn't a control isn't judged. Ratios are WCAG's, cut to two decimals; colours `#rrggbb`. The fix is SwiftUI's (fix round 1): the tokens' advice, and a system colour (the default tint, `.secondary`) replaced with `.foregroundStyle(…)` on the text or `.tint(…)` on a button or link; an icon's names `.foregroundStyle(…)` on the image and `.tint(…)` on its button. Other stacks keep their text. Not covered: text something is drawn over (Flutter's "covered" check), and glass, which refracts what is under it.
    - **Names (ruling of fix round 1: what can be tapped, as Flutter and Compose check).** Buttons, links, toggles, fields and adjustables with no name (a field's prompt names it), or whose name is only their SF Symbol's own. An image is never a name finding: a decorative symbol beside its text ("Leaf") or a row's chevron ("Forward") is read by VoiceOver by the name SwiftUI gives it, and isn't a control; the first version flagged them, once per occurrence. **SwiftUI names a symbol itself**, which the brief didn't expect: `Image(systemName: "gearshape")` in a Button is "Gear Shape", `bell.badge` "Bell With A Notification Badge", `heart.fill` "Love", and in a Hebrew shot "צורת גלגל שיניים". So comparing the name with the raw symbol name finds nothing. SwiftUI gives such an element the symbol's name as its `accessibilityIdentifier` (with "optional" placement, kept when the app adds a label: the star labelled "Favourite" still had `star`), and keeps the texts its label was made from: a symbol's is a `SystemSymbolTextStorage`. A control whose identifier is a symbol (`UIImage(systemName:)`/`NSImage(systemSymbolName:)` knows it) and whose label storage holds only symbols' texts has no name of its own. The storage is read through `Mirror`'s description of SwiftUI's `AccessibilityNode` (`cachedCombinedAttachment`), which isn't API; when it isn't there, the name is compared, in any case, with the raw name and the system's description in the process's language (right in English, not in another language). Such an element's finding names the symbol (`Button(gearshape)`, "The "gearshape" button"), so a shot right to left is the same place. **An older SwiftUI says the raw name.** On CI's iOS 18.5 the unlabelled gear button was called "gearshape" (its identifier still the symbol, so it was found as on iOS 27); on macOS 15 it wasn't found that way at first, so a control's name that is itself a symbol's raw name (`gearshape`, `bell.badge`: lowercase words joined by dots that `UIImage(systemName:)`/`NSImage(systemSymbolName:)` knows) also counts as only the symbol's (`rawSymbol`). The second CI run found it on macOS 15. **What this gets wrong, honestly:** a control the app really calls by a lowercase word that is also a symbol's name (a button labelled "star", or whose `accessibilityIdentifier` is "star") is read as having no name of its own, a false finding. Only controls, since the ruling.
    - **Tap targets (ruling of fix round 1; iOS only, light, phones and tablets).** The first version flagged every stock control under 44 × 44 points: a `TextField` (358 × 23), a switch's row, a bordered prominent `NavigationLink` (131 × 36). The ruling: flag what the app sizes itself, exempt what the system sizes, as Apple does and as the other stacks' stock controls pass. Flagged under 44 points: SwiftUI's own button drawn plain or borderless (`.plain`, `.borderless`, the default `Button("Greet")` 43 × 22), a view with a tap gesture that says it is a button, and a `Link` (29 × 22). Exempt at the size the system gives them: text fields, toggles, sliders and steppers (the adjustable trait, or UIKit's own elements: a stepper's halves are `UIAccessibilityElement`s), pickers and menus (UIKit's `UIKitIconPreferringButton`, and any control that says what it is set to), and a button on a shape of its own (`.bordered`, `.borderedProminent`, `.glass`, `.glassProminent`). **Telling a system-styled button from a plain one:** the tree can't (both are SwiftUI elements with the button trait; only the frame's height differs, 36.33 against 22.33), so the picture does: just inside the middle of the frame's top or bottom edge, the most common colour isn't the one just outside it (a plain button's frame is its label's, on the screen's own background, the same to the pixel). Seen in the probe: bordered, prominent and glass prominent clearly; `.glass` on white only a little (#fcfcfc inside, #ffffff outside, its shadow below), so any difference beyond rounding (2) counts, on either edge. **Every test only exempts (fix round 2), so what Motif can't tell is a missed finding, never a false one:** without the picture (it can't be decoded) no target is judged, and the run says so once ("Motif couldn't read some screenshots' pixels, so their tap targets weren't checked…"); and a text button whose frame is well taller than a line of its text (at least 1.2 times its font size plus 12 points: a bordered button is 36 points high for 17-point text, a plain one 22) is taken for a styled one, when the element says its font and shows no symbol. That second test was needed, and the e2e keeps it so (fix round 3): its planted screen has a `.bordered` "Save" with the theme's tint taken off (`.tint(nil as Color?)`, the neutral fill) on a flat #808080, which the picture can't see and the height test exempts; with the height test switched off, that e2e fails on "The "Save" button is 61×36 px on a phone" (proven on the Mac mini). Tinted bordered buttons (blue, black, indigo, grey) on the same grey were seen by the picture. That second test was needed: probed on the Mac mini (Xcode 27, iOS 27, light) on flat backgrounds of mid grey (#808080), dark grey (#1f1f1f) and blue, `.bordered` on the mid grey read as plain to the picture (its fill nearly the background's) and was flagged; with the height test it isn't. `.glass` and `.glassProminent` were never flagged on any of the three, nor `.bordered` with `.tint(.primary)`. **The false finding left:** a system-styled button the picture doesn't see and whose height doesn't give it away: one whose element says no font, or a small control size (`.controlSize(.small)`, about 28 points for 17-point text, under the height test) on a background its fill matches. Not probed. **Missed findings (what can't be told):** a plain button the app sizes itself to between about 1.2 times its text plus 12 points and 44 points tall (`.frame(height: 40)`, or `.padding(8)`, on a 17-point label) is taken for a styled one by the height test and isn't flagged (fix round 3); a plain button whose frame edge meets another colour (a list's separator 1–3 points away, a tile exactly its size) is taken for a styled one; so may be an icon-only plain button whose filled symbol reaches its frame's edges (`xmark.circle.fill`: in the probe, at 33 × 33, it was still flagged, but a symbol that fills more of its frame's edge wouldn't be); a plain button with its own `.background`, or a custom `ButtonStyle` that draws a shape, is taken for a styled one; a plain button on a gradient or an image may be (its edges differ inside and out); a `UIViewRepresentable` UIButton counts as the system's (it is a `UIView`); and an `.onTapGesture` view without the button trait isn't seen at all. **Menus:** the exemption for a control that says what it is set to was probed with a menu `Picker` only (UIKit's `UIKitIconPreferringButton`, "Choice, One"). A SwiftUI `Menu("Menu") { … }`, probed in fix round 2, is SwiftUI's own button with no value, 43 × 22 for its label, and is flagged like a plain button: its label is what is tapped, and the app sizes it. **What isn't seen:** `Text("Tap me").onTapGesture {}` is exposed as static text, not a button, so it isn't checked unless the app adds `.accessibilityAddTraits(.isButton)`; a link inside a sentence of `Text` is no element of its own (exempt, as WCAG's inline exception). Each flagged kind has its fix: a button's names a system style or `.frame(minWidth: 44, minHeight: 44)` with `.contentShape(Rectangle())` (a tap gesture's too); a link's `.frame(minHeight: 44)` with `.contentShape`. The frame is the element's: a `.frame(width: 30, height: 30)` outside a Button left its element 19 × 21 (the label's size, which is also what takes taps), so the planted button puts the frame inside its label. An unlabelled `Slider` is a name finding ("The element has no name…"), as an adjustable control is tappable. **The Mac gets no tap-target check**, as Flutter and Compose give a desktop size none: it is clicked with a pointer.
    - **Reduced motion (ruling of fix round 1: on the way the app reads it).** The shots that get the motion check have reduce motion on three ways, from before the screen is made until it is torn down. iOS: UIAccessibility's setting, through libAccessibility's `_AXSSetReduceMotionEnabled` in the simulator Motif owns (the same private library as the automation switch, 16), with `UIAccessibility.reduceMotionStatusDidChangeNotification` posted; `UIAccessibility.isReduceMotionEnabled` read back true in the probe, and off again after the shot (and at the start of a launch that has motion shots, as a relaunch after a crash finds the simulator as it was left). The Mac: NSWorkspace's `accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceMotion` in the host, whose getter is replaced once (`method_setImplementation`, the original kept) to answer yes while a shot asks for it, with `accessibilityDisplayOptionsDidChangeNotification` posted; nothing outside the app changes. And on both, SwiftUI's environment value, `.environment(\._accessibilityReduceMotion, true)` on the root: `accessibilityReduceMotion` is get-only, but SwiftUI declares `_accessibilityReduceMotion` with a setter (available since iOS 13 and macOS 10.15, as previews use it), and `@Environment(\.accessibilityReduceMotion)` reads it. **Fenced:** when the system's setting can't be turned on, the motion finding's fix says so. On iOS that is when `_AXSSetReduceMotionEnabled` is missing, or `UIAccessibility.isReduceMotionEnabled` doesn't read it back: the read-back check is iOS's only. On the Mac the read-back goes through the replaced getter itself, so it can't fail; the Mac's fence catches only NSWorkspace having no such method to replace. The fix then says "Motif could turn on only SwiftUI's accessibilityReduceMotion here, not UIAccessibility.isReduceMotionEnabled (NSWorkspace's accessibilityDisplayShouldReduceMotion on the Mac): an animation that reads that was drawn as with reduce motion off, so check it by eye." Detection (fix round 1): 2 s after the picture, every layer's presentation (position, bounds and transform; not opacity, as only movement counts on the web) is sampled six times, 0.1 s apart. The settle loop doesn't wait for running animations' ends (16), so the 2 s are the brief's "2 s after settling". Layers of an activity indicator (the system's spinner) and of a text field (its caret) are left out. ## Consequences

- Motif can draw a SwiftUI app's screens on a Mac with Xcode and an iOS simulator runtime, without touching the developer's project: everything happens in a copy and a simulator Motif deletes. It can't draw them on Windows or Linux, and `motif shot` there must say so (Task 8).
- The app really launches in the simulator as the test's host, before the shots: its launch code (sign-in checks, network calls, analytics) runs once, in the background window. The drawn screens are made again, apart from that launch.
- The harness relies on things that aren't public API: the layout of `Preview` and `WindowGroup` (through `Mirror`), function-type metadata (to call their closures), and the conformance records of `__swift5_proto`. When a layout isn't found, the screen fails with a plain message; when a cast of a closure or a metadata read goes wrong in a way Swift doesn't catch, the app stops, and 12 makes that one screen's failure. The source-based fallback (4) is the way out.
- A screen can't be drawn apart from what the app's scene gives it: the scene's modifiers are given to the root (§16), all but SwiftData's `.modelContainer(for:)`, and a screen that needs what isn't given (that container, what the app's delegate sets up at launch) may be drawn empty, or stop the app (12).
- A run takes half a minute or more even for a small app, most of it booting a simulator and building; shots themselves are a quarter of a second each. A macOS app needs no simulator: a warm run of it takes seconds.
- A macOS app's pictures rely on things that aren't API either (17): AppKit's `_setWindowResolution:` and its active-appearance methods, and `CGWindowListCreateImage`, which the macOS 15 SDK obsoleted. Each has a fallback, and none is silent: a 1× window falls back to AppKit's own 2× drawing, which says glass and materials may be missing; a missing appearance method draws the window greyed, and says so. The real app launches on the Mac for each run, its window shown for about a second, as an app of its own: its own bundle id (suffixed), its home in Motif's work folder, and a defaults domain Motif deletes. It can still reach what the user's account holds outside its home (the Keychain, the user's defaults of other domains, files by absolute path), which a screen's own code may touch.
- Pictures are from the simulator's renderer: the same app may differ slightly between Xcode versions (fonts, control styles: Xcode 27's controls already differ from 16.4's). Motif's tests compare sizes and whether light differs from dark, not pixels.
